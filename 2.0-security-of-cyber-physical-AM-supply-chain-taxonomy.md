## 2. Purpose of the Taxonomy

This paper proposes a structured attack taxonomy for the Additive Manufacturing (AM) supply chain. Its stated purpose is to fill a critical research gap: existing studies had focused on isolated aspects of AM security (e.g., equipment tampering, IP theft) without examining cybersecurity risks holistically across the entire supply chain — from design creation through post-processing and distribution.

The taxonomy is designed to:
1. Categorise threats based on **three key dimensions**: attacker goals, attack targets, and attack methods.
2. Support threat modelling by identifying weak points at each stage of AM production.
3. Enable precise countermeasures by mapping attacker strategies to specific vulnerabilities.
4. Address the full AM supply chain rather than isolated components.

The authors explicitly build upon and extend the framework of Yampolskiy et al. (2016/2018) to incorporate emerging AM-specific supply chain threats, digital twins, distributed manufacturing, and cyber–physical attack vectors.

> **Critical structural note:** Unlike the Yampolskiy et al. (2018) taxonomy (which separated Theft of Technical Data and AM Sabotage into two distinct sub-taxonomies, each with their own attack targets and methods branches), this paper proposes a **single unified three-dimensional taxonomy** with Attack Goals, Attack Targets, and Attack Methods as three co-equal branches. There is no separate "Theft of Technical Data taxonomy" and "AM Sabotage taxonomy" — both of these appear as two of five Attack Goals within the unified structure. Sections 4 and 5 of this document are therefore restructured to faithfully represent this paper's actual organisation. Cross-references to Theft of Technical Data and Sabotage as goal categories are provided within the unified taxonomy.

## 3. Organizing Principles Used by the Authors

### 3.1 Foundation: Yampolskiy et al. (2016) framework

The authors adopt the prior Yampolskiy et al. (2016) framework as a foundation (depicted as Fig. 2 in the paper):

```
Attack Vectors
    → Compromised Elements
        → Manipulations (Influenced Element, Influence)
            (+ Materials and Application Area)
                → Effects (affected elements, Impact)
                    → [intersection with adversarial goal]
                        → Targets / Adversarial Goals
```

According to this framework: "Attackers' success in the AM process depends on the targeted components and the level of control obtained, influencing the tactics used and their potential impact. The impacts of the attack also depend on the type of AM machinery utilised, the materials employed, and the function of the 3D-printed object. Only some of these consequences, nevertheless, support the attacker's objectives. Attack targets or threats are the locations where an attack's results align with the attacker's objectives."

### 3.2 Three-dimensional taxonomy structure

The authors extend this framework into a three-dimensional taxonomy with the following structure (depicted as Fig. 3 in the paper):

```
Attack Goals ←[yellow line]→ Attack Targets ←[green line]→ Attack Methods
```

- **Attack Goals:** The attacker's intended outcomes (what they want to achieve)
- **Attack Targets:** The vulnerabilities within the AM process and supply chain that can be exploited (what they exploit)
- **Attack Methods:** The tactics used to exploit vulnerabilities (how they do it)

### 3.3 Two categories of attack goals

Attack goals are divided into two meta-categories:

1. **Attacker Goals** — deliberate and malicious motivations:
   - *Cyber Goals:* Exploiting vulnerabilities in the digital infrastructure (IP theft, illegal manufacturing, data exfiltration, data infiltration)
   - *Physical Goals:* Deliberate acts of sabotage against physical AM components, equipment, materials, or production lines

2. **Exploited Opportunities** — external factors (not intentional goals) that attackers may exploit:
   - *Natural Disruptions:* Environmental disasters (floods, earthquakes) creating openings when systems are not fully operational
   - *Man-Made Events:* Geopolitical instability, labour strikes, supply chain disruptions creating infiltration opportunities

### 3.4 Relationship lines between taxonomy dimensions

The paper explicitly describes two types of relationships in Fig. 3:
- **Yellow line** (Goal ↔ Target): Specific attack targets can be exploited to achieve attack goals. Example: IP theft can be carried out by targeting part specifications, manufacturing processes, post-processing, or indirect manufacturing.
- **Green solid line** (Target ↔ Method): Multiple attack methods may be used to compromise a particular target.
- **Dotted green line** (Target ↔ Method): An attack method **indirectly** impacts a target. Example: a phishing email gains access to an employee's computer, which then provides access to control software.

### 3.5 Scope of the taxonomy

The taxonomy covers the full AM supply chain across seven stages: Procurement → Sourcing → Manufacturing (Design/CAD, STL Conversion, Slicing, 3D Printing, Post-Processing) → Distribution and Logistics → Delivered Parts → Return.

Unlike the Yampolskiy et al. (2018) taxonomy, this taxonomy:
- Has **broader scope**: covers the entire supply chain including distribution, logistics, and return phases
- Includes **supply chain-specific targets** (export-controlled parts, overproduction, indirect manufacturing dependencies) not present in the 2018 taxonomy
- Operates at a **higher abstraction level** per the authors' own comparison (Table 1)
- Explicitly includes **exploited opportunities** (natural disasters, market instability) as a goal category alongside deliberate attacker goals
- Covers **digital twins** and **distributed manufacturing** as emerging threat surfaces

### 3.6 What this taxonomy explicitly includes that prior taxonomies did not

Per Table 1 of the paper, this taxonomy uniquely addresses:
- Cyber–physical vulnerabilities in the **entire AM supply chain** (not just the AM process)
- Risks from design creation through **post-processing and distribution stages**
- **Distributed manufacturing** and **digital twin** vulnerabilities
- **Counterfeit prevention** and **overproduction risks**
- **IoT-related threats** within the AM supply chain context

### 3.7 CIA Triad Labeling Convention

Each attack method in this document is labeled with one or more CIA triad tags indicating which security properties it threatens:

- **`[C]` — Confidentiality:** The method enables unauthorized access to or disclosure of sensitive information (e.g., IP, design files, process specifications).
- **`[I]` — Integrity:** The method enables unauthorized modification or corruption of data, files, or physical outputs (e.g., altering design geometry, injecting defects into parts).
- **`[A]` — Availability:** The method disrupts or denies access to systems, equipment, or production processes (e.g., ransomware, denial of service, physical destruction of equipment).

A method may carry multiple labels (e.g., `[C, I]`) where it threatens more than one property. Labels are applied based on the primary and secondary effects described in the taxonomy, not worst-case speculation.

### 3.8 CRA Annex Labeling Convention

Each attack method in this document is labeled with one or more CRA Annex tags indicating which specific provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act (EU Regulation 2024/2847) are most directly relevant to preventing, detecting, or mitigating that attack. Labels reference the exact Annex, Part, and paragraph or sub-paragraph.

**Annex I, Part I — Essential Cybersecurity Requirements (Product Properties)**

Products with digital elements shall be designed, developed, and produced to ensure an appropriate level of cybersecurity. The following sub-provisions are used as labels:

- **`[AI.I §1(a)]`** — Products shall be delivered without any known exploitable vulnerabilities.
- **`[AI.I §1(b)]`** — Products shall be delivered with a secure by default configuration, including the ability to reset to original state.
- **`[AI.I §1(c)]`** — Products shall ensure protection from unauthorised access by appropriate control mechanisms, including authentication, identity, or access management systems.
- **`[AI.I §1(d)]`** — Products shall protect the confidentiality of stored, transmitted, or otherwise processed data (personal or other), such as by encrypting relevant data at rest or in transit.
- **`[AI.I §1(e)]`** — Products shall protect the integrity of stored, transmitted, or otherwise processed data, commands, programs, and configuration against unauthorised manipulation or modification, and shall report on corruptions.
- **`[AI.I §1(f)]`** — Products shall process only data that is adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the intended use (data minimisation).
- **`[AI.I §1(g)]`** — Products shall protect the availability of essential functions, including resilience against and mitigation of denial of service attacks.
- **`[AI.I §1(h)]`** — Products shall minimise their own negative impact on the availability of services provided by other devices or networks.
- **`[AI.I §1(i)]`** — Products shall be designed, developed, and produced to limit attack surfaces, including external interfaces.
- **`[AI.I §1(j)]`** — Products shall be designed, developed, and produced to reduce the impact of an incident using appropriate exploitation mitigation mechanisms and techniques.
- **`[AI.I §1(k)]`** — Products shall provide security-related information by recording and/or monitoring relevant internal activity, including access to or modification of data, services, or functions.
- **`[AI.I §1(l)]`** — Products shall ensure that vulnerabilities can be addressed through security updates, including, where applicable, through automatic updates and notification of available updates to users.

**Annex I, Part II — Vulnerability Handling Requirements**

Manufacturers of products with digital elements shall meet the following obligations. The following sub-provisions are used as labels:

- **`[AI.II §1]`** — Manufacturers shall identify and document vulnerabilities and components in the product, including by drawing up a software bill of materials (SBOM) in a commonly used, machine-readable format.
- **`[AI.II §2]`** — Manufacturers shall address and remediate vulnerabilities without delay, including by providing security updates; where technically feasible, security updates shall be provided separately from functionality updates.
- **`[AI.II §3]`** — Manufacturers shall apply effective and regular testing and reviews of product security.
- **`[AI.II §4]`** — Once a security update is available, manufacturers shall disclose information about fixed vulnerabilities, including descriptions, affected products, impacts, severity, and remediation guidance.
- **`[AI.II §5]`** — Manufacturers shall put in place and enforce a policy on coordinated vulnerability disclosure.
- **`[AI.II §6]`** — Manufacturers shall take measures to facilitate sharing of information about potential vulnerabilities in their product and in third-party components used in it.
- **`[AI.II §7]`** — Manufacturers shall provide mechanisms to securely distribute updates for products with digital elements to ensure timely remediation of exploitable vulnerabilities.
- **`[AI.II §8]`** — Manufacturers shall ensure that security patches or updates are disseminated without delay and, unless justified, free of charge — including, where technically feasible, via automatic updates.

**Annex II — Information and Instructions to Users**

- **`[AII]`** — Annex II sets out the information and instructions that must accompany products with digital elements. This label applies where an attack succeeds in part because end-users lack the mandatory security guidance, configuration instructions, or risk information that Annex II requires the manufacturer to provide.

**Annex III — Important Products with Digital Elements**

- **`[AIII-I]`** — The attack targets a Class I important product (e.g., identity management software, password managers, network monitoring tools, routers, switches, microbrowsers, SCADA systems), for which the CRA requires the manufacturer to follow a specific conformity assessment route (Module A — internal control or a harmonised standard).
- **`[AIII-II]`** — The attack targets a Class II important product (e.g., OS for servers/desktops/mobile, hypervisors, firewalls, intrusion detection/prevention systems, tamper-resistant microprocessors), for which the CRA requires a stricter conformity route (Module B+C EU-type examination, or Module H full quality assurance).

**Annex IV — Critical Products with Digital Elements**

- **`[AIV]`** — The attack targets a critical product (hardware security modules, smart meter gateways, secure elements, smartcard ICs, root CA hardware), requiring mandatory third-party conformity assessment under the strictest CRA conformity route.

A method may carry multiple labels (e.g., `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.II §2]`) where more than one specific provision is implicated. Labels identify the CRA obligations most directly relevant to the attack vector — not every conceivable regulatory touchpoint.

## 4. Taxonomy Dimension 1: Attack Goals

> **Source:** Section 4.1, Table 2, and Fig. 3 of the paper.

The taxonomy defines five primary attack goals. These represent the attacker's intended outcomes and serve as the left-most column of Fig. 3.

### 4.1 Overview

| Attack Goal | CIA | CRA | Related Threats (from Table 2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intellectual Property Compromise | `[C]` | `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(i)]` | Design theft, counterfeiting, reverse engineering, industrial espionage, unlicensed reproduction |
| Unauthorised Part Production | `[I]` | `[AI.I §1(b), AI.I §1(c), AII]` | Counterfeiting, unlicensed reproduction, bypassing regulatory compliance, evasion of supply chain controls, grey market operations |
| Data Breach and Manipulation | `[C, I]` | `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(k)]` | Unauthorised access, IP theft, gaining insight into quality and performance, sabotage, accessing sensitive information |
| Sabotage | `[I, A]` | `[AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(j), AI.I §1(k)]` | Compromising object integrity, inducing weaknesses, tampering with material properties, disrupting the production process, targeting critical components, concealing defects |
| Denial of Service | `[A]` | `[AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(h)]` | Environmental disasters, power outages, material contamination, temperature and humidity fluctuations, transportation challenges, supply chain vulnerabilities |

### 4.2 Goal 1: Intellectual Property Compromise `[C]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(i)]`

IP in the AM context includes legal rights granted to creators for innovative designs and inventions. Unauthorised access to IP is a critical goal for attackers seeking financial gain or competitive advantage.

Sub-threats within this goal:
- **CAD File Theft:** Attackers exploit vulnerabilities in CAD systems to gain unauthorised access to proprietary 3D models and design files; allows copying or modification of physical specifications, causing economic losses and market share harm.
- **Counterfeiting and Unlicensed Reproduction:** Counterfeiters reproduce 3D-printed objects without permission, flooding markets with fake products that fail to meet quality standards. Unlicensed reproduction replicates objects without proper rights.
- **Reverse Engineering:** Attackers use non-destructive methods (e.g., 3D scanning) on physical copies to create digital models, uncover proprietary design features, manufacturing processes, and material compositions, enabling unauthorised copies or derivative works.
- **Industrial Espionage:** Competitors or malicious actors hack R&D databases, steal design files, or infiltrate companies to access proprietary AM-related information, gaining competitive advantage while harming the affected business's reputation and financials.

### 4.3 Goal 2: Unauthorised Part Production `[I]` `[AI.I §1(b), AI.I §1(c), AII]`

Attacker aims to use AM capabilities to create items prohibited or restricted by law, regulations, or ethical standards.

Sub-threats:
- **Counterfeiting:** Production of counterfeit components that imitate real components; posed for sale, deceiving buyers and threatening device operation and security.
- **Unlicensed Reproduction:** Illegal replicas of proprietary parts, violating the original manufacturers' IP rights.
- **Bypassing Regulatory Compliance:** Manufacturing AM components without required licenses and certifications in regulated industries (aerospace, medicine), resulting in safety risks and legal consequences.
- **Evasion of Supply Chain Controls:** Using AM's decentralised and localised production capability to manufacture illegal parts outside permitted manufacturing routes, making it challenging to identify the source.
- **Grey Market Operations:** Running a grey market business creating parts without original producers' or authorised distributors' consent; sold at lower prices, affecting reputable suppliers' income and threatening the genuine goods market.

### 4.4 Goal 3: Data Breach and Manipulation `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(k)]`

Sensitive information about designs, production methods, and IP is valuable; data intrusion and exfiltration are essential attack goals.

Sub-threats:
- **Data Infiltration:** Attackers break into AM systems to access valuable data including CAD files, STL models, slicing settings, G-Code, and confidential design knowledge. Methods include software vulnerabilities, phishing emails, or poor authentication procedures.
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** AM networks are infiltrated to steal valuable IP (unique designs, manufacturing techniques, product innovations) for financial gain, unauthorised production, or sale to competitors or counterfeiters.
- **Design and Process Information:** Malicious individuals access data relating to design parameters, material specifications, and printing settings to learn about product quality, performance, or distinct features — enabling unauthorised replication or modification.
- **Tampering:** Attackers break into AM systems and change design files, slicing parameters, or G-Code instructions, threatening the integrity, safety, or function of 3D-printed products by introducing malicious components.
- **Data Exfiltration:** After breaching AM systems, attackers exfiltrate data to outside servers. Can be challenging to spot and may happen gradually, potentially exposing important data for an extended time.

### 4.5 Goal 4: Sabotage `[I, A]` `[AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(j), AI.I §1(k)]`

Attackers aim to compromise the integrity and functionality of 3D-printed objects or disrupt manufacturing. Particularly severe in industries where safety and reliability are paramount.

Sub-threats:
- **Compromising Parts' Integrity:** Adjustments to end-effector translational instructions could alter the amplitude envelope and instantaneous phase response, signalling compromise in the AM system.
- **Introducing Weaknesses:** Sabotage attacks introduce hidden weaknesses or vulnerabilities in 3D-printed objects that remain undetected during quality control, leading to unexpected failures or reduced durability.
- **Tampering with Material Properties:** Planned sabotage attacks on composite material parts aim to minimise the "footprint" of manipulations; can be optimised by two criteria (demonstrated against a spar, a load-bearing component of an aeroplane wing).
- **Disrupting Production Processes:** Attacks target the AM manufacturing process itself, causing production delays, material wastage, and financial losses by interfering with 3D printers, software systems, or network connectivity.
- **Targeting Critical Components:** In industries using AM for safety-critical components (aerospace, medical, automotive), attackers focus on sabotaging specific parts to cause system failures or accidents.
- **Concealing Defects:** Manufacturers or attackers make the printed object appear visually acceptable despite hidden weaknesses or compromised structural integrity, to pass visual inspections and quality control checks or deceive users.

### 4.6 Goal 5: Denial of Service `[A]` `[AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(h)]`

Disruption of service availability causes severe operational disruptions, financial losses, and competitive advantage to the attacker. Can stem from both natural disasters and human-made threats.

Sub-threats:
- **Natural Disasters:** Earthquakes, floods, hurricanes, or wildfires directly damage AM facilities (printers, raw materials, infrastructure), leading to prolonged downtime, production delays, and significant recovery costs.
- **Power Outages:** Disruptions caused by storms, grid failures, or other events interrupt the AM process (requiring stable power for temperature and environmental conditions), leading to incomplete prints, material wastage, or equipment damage.
- **Material Contamination:** Environmental disruptions lead to contamination of 3D printing materials (e.g., plastics and metal powders absorbing moisture or particulate matter from air), affecting material properties and quality of printed objects.
- **Temperature and Humidity Fluctuations:** Fluctuations adversely affect quality of 3D-printed products. Reactive metal powders (e.g., Ti-6Al-4V) are highly sensitive to humidity. Heat buildup from lasers or electron beams influences final product quality.

## 5. Taxonomy Dimension 2: Attack Targets

> **Source:** Section 4.2, Table 3, and Fig. 3 of the paper. The paper also provides Fig. 4, a matrix mapping research papers to the attack targets they address.

### 5.1 Overview of all attack targets and associated potential threats

### 5.2 Target 1: 3D Design and Part Specification `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e)]`

The geometrical and material characteristics of a 3D-printed object — dimensions, shape, orientation, resolution, mechanical properties, and printing material — hold sensitive data that, if exposed, could be exploited to duplicate, modify, or sabotage the object.

Sub-vulnerabilities exploitable by attackers:
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** Part specifications include proprietary CAD files, STL models, and G-Code instructions; attackers steal to gain insights into designs, techniques, and material compositions for replication or modification without authorisation.
- **Counterfeiting:** Attackers target part specifications to produce counterfeit parts resembling genuine products for sale, deceiving consumers and causing safety risks.
- **Design Manipulation:** Attackers access part specifications to manipulate design files, slicing parameters, or G-Code, introducing subtle changes or defects that compromise structural integrity and functionality.
- **Sabotage:** Attackers use specifications of critical parts to identify weaknesses in the design or manufacturing process, introducing defects that may go unnoticed during quality control.
- **Unauthorised Production:** Specifications enable attackers to produce parts without proper authorisation or compliance with safety and regulatory standards, leading to safety hazards, system failures, or accidents.
- **Supply Chain Disruptions:** Compromising part specifications disrupts the supply chain by affecting availability and quality of printed parts; attackers may alter specifications to introduce delays, inconsistencies, or inferior product quality.

### 5.3 Target 2: Manufacturing Process `[C, I, A]` `[AI.I §1(a), AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(j), AI.I §1(k)]`

The manufacturing stage encompasses design-to-production workflow; AM systems include software managing this workflow and hardware (3D printers) building products layer by layer.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Process Tampering:** Attackers alter the printing process's critical settings, parameters, or materials, compromising quality and structural integrity.
- **Material Substitution:** Replacement of authorised AM materials with subpar or inferior ones, leading to compromised performance and safety hazards.
- **Slicing Software Manipulation:** Targeting slicing software to inject malicious code or manipulate settings, leading to flawed 3D printing and compromised quality.
- **Software and Firmware Vulnerabilities:** Exploiting vulnerabilities in AM control software/firmware to gain unauthorised access, disrupt printing, or compromise final output.
- **Injection of Defects:** Introducing defects into the AM process or modifying design files to create weakened/damaged 3D-printed objects; defects may not be easily detectable.
- **Post-Processing Compromises:** Tampering with cleaning, curing, or surface treatment procedures, introducing defects or safety risks.
- **System Access:** Gaining unauthorised access and control over 3D printers, leading to production disruptions, data theft, or sabotage.
- **Infiltration of Material Supply Chain:** Compromising the supply chain of AM materials, introducing contaminated or low-quality materials into the manufacturing process.

### 5.4 Target 3: Post-Processing Stage `[I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(k), AII]`

Post-processing (heat treatment, surface smoothing, quality inspections, cleaning, polishing, finishing treatments) presents an opportunity for adversaries to introduce undetectable defects, alter material properties, or bypass quality control measures.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Surface Contamination:** Introducing contaminants or foreign substances during post-processing steps (cleaning, polishing, surface treatments) affecting mechanical properties, aesthetics, or biocompatibility.
- **Quality Degradation:** Intentionally performing subpar or inadequate post-processing to degrade the quality of printed parts, leading to functional issues or reduced performance.
- **Tampering with Surface Treatments:** Applying incorrect or unsuitable coatings or treatments that compromise the intended functionality or aesthetics.
- **Structural Modifications:** Using post-processing steps to introduce undiscovered weaknesses, structural modifications, or other issues that endanger parts' integrity or functionality.
- **Introducing Hidden Components:** Using post-processing to introduce hidden components that compromise the intended functionality or cause safety risks.
- **Substituting Post-Processing Materials:** Replacing authorised post-processing materials with inferior or non-conforming ones (incorrect finishes or coatings that fall short of necessary criteria).
- **Data Alteration:** Manipulating the data or settings applied during post-processing, resulting in unexpected or compromised outcomes.
- **Sabotaging Post-Processing Equipment:** Targeting post-processing equipment to cause malfunctions, disruptions, or delays, impacting timely delivery or quality.

### 5.5 Target 4: Indirect Manufacturing Dependencies `[C, I, A]` `[AI.I §1(a), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.II §1, AI.II §6]`

Attackers exploit vulnerabilities in indirect manufacturing dependencies — the weak connections in third-party suppliers, logistics, and environmental controls — to disrupt operations, steal intellectual property, and compromise product quality. Entry points include counterfeit materials, design tampering, and production delays. Cyberattacks on software, power infrastructure, and post-processing systems can lead to undetected defects. Human factors (insider threats, social engineering) further amplify these risks.

*(The paper describes this target at a higher level of abstraction than the others, without enumerating individual sub-vulnerabilities in the same detail.)*

### 5.6 Target 5: Intellectual Property Right (labeled "Infringement" in Table 3) `[C]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(k)]`

Attackers may seek to violate intellectual property rights or patents by reproducing and distributing copyrighted designs or patented products without proper authorisation, with severe legal and financial consequences.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** Targeting AM systems to steal valuable IP including CAD files, STL models, and G-Code instructions related to copyrighted designs or patented products.
- **Counterfeiting:** Producing counterfeit products closely resembling genuine ones; counterfeit parts may be sold as genuine, creating safety risks if quality/safety standards are not met.
- **Unauthorised Reproduction:** Using AM technology to reproduce and distribute copyrighted designs or patented products without obtaining licences or permissions.
- **Design Piracy:** Copying and reproducing unique and innovative designs without authorisation; AM makes it easy to replicate and distribute pirated designs.
- **Patent Infringement:** Reproducing patented products using AM technology, directly violating patent rights (patents protect specific features or functionalities).
- **Brand Dilution:** Counterfeit or pirated products harm the reputation and trust of the original brand; consumers associate poor quality or safety issues with the genuine brand.

### 5.7 Target 6: Unauthorised Overproduction `[I, A]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(k)]`

Attackers produce excess 3D-printed items beyond legitimate orders, leading to financial losses, resource depletion, and legal complications.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Resource Wastage:** Overproduction consumes materials, energy, and other resources unnecessarily, increasing costs and environmental impact.
- **Inventory Surplus:** Producing more than required creates inventory surplus, tying up valuable storage space and capital with increased carrying costs.
- **Loss of Value:** Overproduced items may lose their value over time, especially if outdated or replaced by newer designs; excess inventory may become obsolete.
- **Opportunity Costs:** Resources diverted to overproduction cause missed opportunities for more lucrative or urgent production demands.
- **Legal Implications:** Overproduction of copyrighted or patented designs can lead to legal issues and potential financial penalties.
- **Reputation Damage:** Customers may perceive the AM facility as inefficient or wasteful, leading to loss of trust and potential business opportunities.

---

### 5.8 Target 7: Export-Controlled Parts `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AII, AI.II §1]`

Export-controlled parts are susceptible components, technologies, or products subject to strict regulations and restrictions on export and transfer to foreign countries — often with military, defence, or dual-use applications.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Unauthorised Transfer:** Attackers bypass export control regulations by producing export-controlled parts using AM and transferring/exporting them to restricted destinations without authorisation.
- **Reverse Engineering:** AM's ability to reproduce intricate and complex parts makes it susceptible to reverse engineering attacks; attackers use AM to create replicas and circumvent export control restrictions.
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** Producing export-controlled parts may involve accessing sensitive design files and specifications; attackers target these assets to steal valuable information.
- **Distribution Networks:** Attackers use AM to establish clandestine production and distribution networks for export-controlled parts, facilitating unauthorised transfer to restricted entities or countries.
- **Dual-Use Technologies:** Some AM materials and processes have civilian and military applications; attackers exploit these dual-use capabilities to produce and move export-controlled parts across borders without oversight.
- **Supply Chain Vulnerabilities:** The AM supply chain's multiple stakeholders create susceptibility to vulnerabilities that can be exploited to access export-controlled parts and specifications.

### 5.9 Target 8: Internally Restricted Parts `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(k)]`

Internally restricted parts refer to components or objects banned or restricted within a specific organisation or facility due to safety, security, or legal concerns.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Unauthorised Production:** Attackers produce internally prohibited parts using AM technology, circumventing the organisation's restrictions and regulations, leading to items that pose safety risks or violate internal policies.
- **Sabotage:** Attackers intentionally produce prohibited parts with defects or weaknesses that compromise the functionality or safety of the final product; sabotaged parts introduced into manufacturing to cause harm.
- **Insider Threats:** Internal personnel with access to AM systems and data may attempt to produce prohibited parts intentionally or unintentionally (negligence, lack of awareness, or malicious intent).
- **Regulatory Non-Compliance:** Production of internally prohibited parts may violate industry regulations or internal policies, leading to legal consequences and reputational damage.
- **Intellectual Property Concerns:** Internally prohibited parts may include sensitive designs or proprietary information; attackers may target these to steal IP or confidential data.
- **Brand Reputation:** Discovery of internally prohibited parts produced within the organisation can severely damage brand reputation and erode trust.

### 5.10 Target 9: CAD Model Integrity `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.II §1]`

CAD models in AM are valuable assets containing the digital representation of 3D objects, including shape, dimensions, and features; they serve as the foundation for the entire AM process.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** CAD models represent proprietary designs, innovative products, or trade secrets; attackers target these models to steal valuable IP for reproduction or counterfeiting.
- **Unauthorised Reproduction:** Attackers obtain CAD models without authorisation to produce unauthorised copies, leading to IP infringement.
- **Sabotage:** Attackers tamper with CAD models to introduce defects, weaknesses, or changes that compromise the functionality or safety of 3D-printed objects.
- **Design Piracy:** CAD models for unique and innovative designs are susceptible to copying and reproduction without authorisation, undermining original designers' efforts.
- **Supply Chain Vulnerabilities:** CAD models are often shared among various stakeholders (designers, manufacturers, service providers); attackers exploit vulnerabilities in the supply chain to gain unauthorised access.
- **Counterfeit Production:** By obtaining CAD models of branded or copyrighted products, attackers produce counterfeit items that closely resemble the originals, deceiving consumers and causing financial losses.
- **Design Leakage:** CAD models may be inadvertently leaked or shared without proper security, making them susceptible to unauthorised access or theft.

### 5.11 Target 10: STL Phase Integrity `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.II §1]`

STL (STereoLithography) files are a common format in AM representing the surface geometry of 3D models as triangular facets; an essential component of the AM process.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** STL files contain digital representations of 3D objects including shapes and features; attackers target these files to steal valuable IP related to innovative designs or proprietary products.
- **Unauthorised Reproduction:** Attackers obtain STL files without authorisation to reproduce or counterfeit the original designs, leading to IP infringement.
- **Design Tampering:** Attackers tamper with STL files to introduce defects, alterations, or modifications to the 3D model, resulting in flawed or compromised objects.
- **Design Piracy:** STL files for unique and proprietary designs are susceptible to copying and reproduction without permission.
- **Counterfeit Production:** By obtaining STL files of branded or copyrighted products, attackers produce counterfeit items that closely resemble the originals.
- **Supply Chain Vulnerabilities:** STL files are often shared among various stakeholders; attackers exploit vulnerabilities in the supply chain to gain unauthorised access.
- **Design Leakage:** STL files may be inadvertently leaked or shared without proper security, making them susceptible to unauthorised access or theft.

### 5.12 Target 11: Tool-Path Execution `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(k)]`

Tool paths refer to the specific paths the 3D printer's print head or nozzle follows while depositing material to create each layer. Generated by slicing software from the 3D model's design; critical to accuracy and quality of the final printed object.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Tampering with Tool Paths:** Attackers tamper with generated tool paths to introduce defects, errors, or modifications to the printing process, compromising the integrity and quality of the 3D-printed object.
- **Sabotage:** Tool path manipulation leads to intentional sabotage; attackers modify tool paths to create weak spots or structural vulnerabilities, leading to failure or reduced performance.
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** Tool paths reveal information about the manufacturing process and design of the 3D object; attackers target tool paths to extract valuable information.
- **Counterfeit Production:** By gaining access to tool paths used for a specific object, attackers attempt to create counterfeit versions of the original product.
- **Supply Chain Vulnerabilities:** Tool paths may be shared among different stakeholders in the AM supply chain, making them susceptible to unauthorised access or potential breaches.
- **Infiltration of Malicious Code:** Attackers inject malicious code or instructions into the tool path generation process, leading to layer misalignment or inconsistent material deposition during printing.

### 5.13 Target 12: Final Manufactured Part `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(e), AII]`

Manufactured parts are attractive attack targets due to their potential impact on safety, functionality, and reputation. Attackers may target the manufactured parts themselves or use the AM process to produce malicious parts.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Counterfeit Parts:** Attackers use AM to produce counterfeit parts closely resembling genuine ones; sold or used in products without meeting quality and safety standards, leading to safety risks and product failures.
- **Substandard Quality:** Attackers intentionally produce parts with substandard quality using inferior materials or improper manufacturing parameters, compromising overall performance and durability.
- **Design Alterations:** AM allows easy modifications; attackers alter designs to introduce defects or weaknesses, leading to failure or underperformance of the products in which parts are used.
- **Critical Component Manipulation:** Attackers target critical components in aerospace, defence, or medical devices, manipulating design or manufacturing process to introduce vulnerabilities or sabotage functionality of essential components.
- **Supply Chain Vulnerabilities:** AM involves various supply chain stakeholders; attackers exploit vulnerabilities to introduce malicious parts, resulting in compromised products and damaged reputations.
- **Intellectual Property Theft:** Attackers target manufactured parts to gain unauthorised access to proprietary designs and specifications.
- **Infiltration of Harmful Elements:** Attackers use AM to produce parts embedded with harmful elements or substances, posing serious safety risks to end-users or the environment.

### 5.14 Target 13: AM Equipment `[C, I, A]` `[AI.I §1(a), AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(i), AI.I §1(k), AI.II §2]`

AM equipment plays a crucial role in the AM process; attackers may target the equipment or exploit vulnerabilities to disrupt or compromise the AM process.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Physical Tampering:** Attackers physically tamper with AM equipment (altering hardware, components, settings) to affect the quality or accuracy of 3D-printed objects.
- **Unauthorised Access:** Attackers gain unauthorised access to the AM equipment's control systems or software, allowing manipulation of the printing process, changes to settings, or introduction of malicious code.
- **Sabotage:** Deliberate sabotage of AM equipment disrupts manufacturing or produces flawed/defective parts, resulting in financial losses and reputational damage.
- **Theft or Vandalism:** AM equipment represents significant investment; attackers target equipment for theft or vandalism, resulting in financial losses and operational disruptions.
- **Data Breach:** AM equipment may store sensitive data (design files, material specifications, manufacturing processes); attackers breach equipment security to access and steal this information.
- **Supply Chain Vulnerabilities:** AM equipment may be part of a larger supply chain; attackers exploit weaknesses to compromise the equipment and introduce malicious elements into the manufacturing process.
- **Production Interruption:** Disrupting AM equipment's operation leads to production delays or stoppages, causing financial losses and impacting project timelines.

### 5.15 Target 14: AM Production Environment `[I, A]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(i), AII]`

The environment refers to the physical surroundings and conditions in which the AM process occurs. While not a direct target for attacks, it can be vulnerable to risks and challenges impacting the AM process and quality.

Sub-vulnerabilities:
- **Temperature and Humidity:** Ambient temperature and humidity affect material properties and the printing process; extreme levels outside recommended range lead to warping, shrinkage, or poor adhesion of layers.
- **Contaminants:** Particulates, dust, and other contaminants negatively impact the printing process and integrity of final parts, causing defects, surface imperfections, or compromised mechanical properties.
- **Power Supply Stability:** A reliable power supply is essential; fluctuations or disruptions lead to printing errors, interruptions, or equipment damage.
- **Ventilation and Fumes:** Some AM materials emit fumes or gases during printing (especially certain resins or filaments); adequate ventilation is necessary to mitigate health hazards.
- **Noise and Vibrations:** AM machines generate noise and vibrations during printing; excessive levels can lead to operational issues.
- **Security and Access Control:** Physical security of the AM environment is crucial to prevent unauthorised access or tampering with equipment, materials, or designs.
- **Emergency Preparedness:** Well-defined emergency procedures and protocols are important for addressing equipment malfunctions, power outages, or safety hazards.

### 6.2 Method 1: Reverse Engineering of AM Design `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(i)]`

Involves the unauthorised gaining and analysis of a 3D object or product to obtain its design and manufacturing information. Attackers utilise reverse engineering methods to:
- Modify original designs or make unauthorised copies
- Learn sensitive information about proprietary technologies
- Illegally copy and disseminate blueprints for 3D objects using digital reproductions (causing financial harm and reducing competitive ability of legitimate manufacturers)
- Manufacture counterfeit 3D components that appear genuine (deceiving buyers, customers, and AM industry stakeholders)
- Duplicate existing designs without knowledge or permission of original developer, leading to illicit production and market saturation
- Alter design of 3D objects by adding flaws, weaknesses, or covert vulnerabilities that jeopardise the final object's functionality or safety

### 6.3 Method 2: Cyber Intrusion on AM Equipment `[C, I, A]` `[AI.I §1(a), AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(j), AI.II §2]`

Attacks on control systems and AM hardware via cyberspace; can substantially impact the quality of printed products and the overall AM process.

Specific techniques:
- **Illegal Access:** Obtaining illegal entrance into control systems of AM equipment using software flaws or lax authentication procedures; altering printing settings, interfering with operations, or controlling printing processes.
- **Malware Injection:** Introducing malware into AM equipment control systems that can interfere with equipment operation, steal sensitive data, or spread to other network sections.
- **Data Breach/IP Theft:** Targeting AM equipment storage of sensitive data (design files, manufacturing parameters, material specifications); exploiting security to gain unlawful access potentially resulting in IP theft or unauthorised output.
- **Ransomware:** Locking down control systems of AM equipment and demanding payment to unlock; resulting in supply chain disruption, production delays, and monetary losses.
- **Tool Path Manipulation:** Altering slicing software's tool paths, creating faulty or hacked 3D-printed objects.
- **Supply Chain Infiltration:** Breaching software or firmware used in AM equipment, exploiting vulnerabilities to influence the printing process.

### 6.4 Method 3: Physical Sabotage on AM Equipment `[I, A]` `[AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.I §1(i), AI.I §1(k)]`

Directly disrupting or tampering with hardware, components, or operating circumstances to obstruct operations, introduce flaws, or make the device malfunction.

Specific techniques:
- **Hardware Tampering:** Physically tampering with hardware (changing sensors, motors, actuators), resulting in mechanical failures during printing.
- **Critical Component Sabotage:** Injuring cooling or heating components, causing uneven material deposition and harming structural integrity of printed items.
- **Contamination:** Introduction of unwanted objects, trash, or contaminants into the AM equipment, interfering with printing process and lowering quality.
- **Printing Parameter Modification:** Changing layer thickness, print speed, or temperature settings, influencing the printing process to add flaws or blemishes.
- **Overloading:** Physically overloading AM machinery beyond its intended capability, resulting in mechanical stress, machinery failure, or safety risks.
- **Vandalism/Destruction:** Sabotage or purposeful destruction of AM equipment, rendering it useless and incurring significant financial losses.

### 6.5 Method 4: Bypassing Digital and Physical Protection `[C, I]` `[AI.I §1(a), AI.I §1(b), AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(j), AI.II §2]`

Protection removal — getting beyond security precautions and safeguards protecting the AM process, design files, and intellectual property.

Specific techniques:
- **IP Protection Removal:** Targeting defences that shield important resources like design files, secret manufacturing techniques, or trade secrets, then stealing and using this information to copy or modify designs without required authority.
- **Access Control Bypass:** Getting past access control systems used in AM operations to prevent unwanted equipment and design information access.
- **Digital Signature Removal/Modification:** Changing or erasing digital signatures used to confirm legitimacy and integrity of design files, compromising integrity of 3D-printed goods or making unauthorised alterations.
- **Encryption Key Extraction:** Obtaining encryption keys to decrypt sensitive data and gain access to critical data or command and control systems.
- **Firmware Modification:** Attacking the firmware of AM hardware (essential to regulating and assuring correctness of the printing process) to introduce weaknesses or bypass safety measures.
- **Copy Protection Removal:** Disabling or removing copy protection techniques used in certain AM software to avoid unauthorised reproduction, allowing designs to be copied and shared without required authorisation.

### 6.6 Method 5: Intellectual Property Theft `[C]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(i), AI.I §1(k)]`

Theft of IP relating to 3D designs, manufacturing techniques, or trade secrets; a prominent AM attack technique causing monetary losses, reputational harm, and loss of competitive advantage.

Specific techniques:
- **3D Design File Theft:** Stealing CAD software-produced 3D design files (the product's blueprint) to copy or modify designs without required authority.
- **Manufacturing Process Theft:** Stealing exclusive manufacturing procedures that give companies a competitive advantage, used to create identical goods or learn about production capabilities.
- **Trade Secret Extraction:** Extracting and using trade secrets about material compositions, post-processing methods, or unique manufacturing processes to acquire competitive edge.
- **Corporate Espionage:** Infiltrating AM operations to obtain access to sensitive IP through hacking, social engineering, or insider threats.
- **Reverse Engineering for IP Extraction:** Disassembling 3D-printed items to reproduce the original design files; physically analysing the object to understand its structure and qualities, then creating a digital clone. Enables illegal reproduction and distribution, causing financial losses for original creators.

### 6.7 Method 6: Counterfeiting Part Protection `[I]` `[AI.I §1(b), AI.I §1(e), AII]`

Attackers use 3D printing technology to create unlicensed copies of real things. Risks to customers, manufacturers, and the market:
- Creating false versions of actual products that closely imitate appearance and functioning without necessary authorisation or licencing.
- Using subpar materials or poor manufacturing techniques that compromise quality and durability (products that may not perform up to expectations with shorter lifespan).
- Not subjecting counterfeit goods to the same thorough testing and safety regulations as real goods, posing consumer safety risks.
- Damaging legitimate manufacturers' reputations — customers who encounter problems with counterfeits can link the problem to the real brand.
- Lowering market demand for genuine goods, negatively affecting sales and bottom line for legitimate manufacturers.

### 6.8 Method 7: Exploitation of Supply Chain Weaknesses `[C, I, A]` `[AI.I §1(a), AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(g), AI.II §1, AI.II §6]`

Attackers compromise weak points in the supply chain to obtain access, introduce harmful components, or obstruct the production process.

Specific techniques:
- **Third-Party Supplier Compromise:** Exploiting flaws in vendors' systems or procedures to inject faulty or malicious materials into the supply chain, producing inferior or dangerous 3D-printed goods.
- **Cyberattacks on Suppliers:** Using phishing, malware, or ransomware to compromise suppliers' infrastructure or networks, gaining access to private information, business secrets, or design files.
- **Design File Interception:** Intercepting design files being stored or sent in the supply chain to develop fake goods or alter original designs without authorisation.
- **Counterfeiting Material:** Introducing fake goods that do not adhere to quality standards or legal criteria, undermining the supply chain (using subpar materials instead of genuine ones).
- **Supply Chain Operation Sabotage:** Carrying out physical or cyber attacks on vital infrastructure, causing production delays, monetary losses, and reputational damage.

### 6.9 Method 8: Tempering with Part Specification `[I]` `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(e), AI.I §1(k)]`

Adjusting or changing the specifications or design parameters of 3D-printed components throughout the design or manufacturing process.

Specific techniques:
- **Design Specification Alteration:** Changing important dimensions, geometries, or material compositions, compromising structural integrity, possibly causing failure during use.
- **Material Substitution:** Using inferior or inappropriate replacements for the supplied material, resulting in weak or unreliable 3D-printed parts.
- **Tolerance Modification:** Modifying the part's specified tolerances, resulting in improper fits or assembly; parts with wrong tolerances may fail or malfunction.
- **Surface Finish Alteration:** Changing the surface finish set for 3D-printed parts, affecting appearance, mechanical capabilities, or functionality; purposefully adding textured or uneven surfaces.
- **Feature Addition or Removal:** Modifying design to add extraneous components (raising production complexity and expense) or remove crucial functionality (damaging the item).
- **Assembly Manipulation:** Altering the part's interface or mating features to prevent correct integration or assembly when the 3D-printed item is meant to be assembled with other parts.

## 9. Taxonomy Notes and Interpretation Constraints

### 9.1 This taxonomy is broader in scope than Yampolskiy et al. (2018)

Per the authors' own comparison in Table 1, their taxonomy:
- Covers the **entire AM supply chain** (design through return/recycling) rather than just the AM manufacturing process
- Includes **cyber–physical attack vectors** not addressed by prior work
- Addresses **distributed manufacturing** and **digital twin** vulnerabilities
- Captures distinct vulnerabilities like **counterfeit prevention**, **overproduction risks**, and **export control** concerns
- Specifically incorporates **supply chain logistics** (distribution, delivered parts, return phases)

However, the authors also acknowledge (Table 1 column "Relation to our work") that Yampolskiy et al. (2018) focused on AM processes but did not cover supply chain or cyber–physical aspects comprehensively — and this paper operates at a **higher abstraction level** than the 2018 taxonomy.

### 9.2 No separation of "Theft" and "Sabotage" sub-taxonomies

Unlike Yampolskiy et al. (2018), which provided two completely separate sub-taxonomies (one for Theft of Technical Data, one for AM Sabotage, each with their own targets and methods branches), this paper uses **one unified taxonomy** where "Intellectual Property Compromise" and "Sabotage" appear as two of five Attack Goals. There are no separate sets of targets or methods for each goal — the same 14 targets and 14 methods apply across all goals.

### 9.3 Goals include exploited opportunities, not just deliberate attacker intentions

The "Denial of Service" attack goal is treated unusually: it includes natural disasters, power outages, material contamination, and temperature/humidity fluctuations as sub-threats — even though these are not deliberate attack methods. The paper explicitly categorises these as "Exploited Opportunities" (a meta-category alongside "Attacker Goals") where attackers take advantage of external disruptions to compromise the system when it is most vulnerable. This means the taxonomy's goal dimension spans from fully deliberate (IP Compromise) to opportunistic exploitation of environmental events (DoS via natural disaster).

### 9.4 Many attack targets are cross-cutting

Several attack targets (e.g., "Indirect Manufacturing Dependencies," "Intellectual Property Right," "Unauthorised Overproduction," "Export-Controlled Parts," "Internally Restricted Parts") are not specific to a single stage of the AM workflow. They are cross-cutting concerns that span the entire supply chain. This differs from the more stage-specific target organisation of the Yampolskiy 2018 taxonomy.

### 9.5 Paper provides reference-to-taxonomy mapping figures

The paper provides two figures (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5) that map cited research papers to specific taxonomy nodes:
- **Fig. 4:** Maps research papers to Attack Targets (green circle = directly addressed, yellow circle = indirectly addressed)
- **Fig. 5:** Maps research papers to Attack Methods (green circle = directly addressed, yellow circle = indirectly addressed)

These figures serve as a literature survey overlay on the taxonomy and are included to show which taxonomy nodes have and have not been addressed in the literature. They are not part of the taxonomy structure itself.

### 9.6 The paper notes "tempering" rather than "tampering" in method names

The paper uses "Tempering with Part Specification" and "Tempering with Post-Processing Procedures" — this appears to be a non-standard usage of "tempering" where "tampering" would be the standard cybersecurity term. This document preserves the paper's exact wording ("Tempering") faithfully.

### 9.7 Attack methods 8–14 are partially redundant with each other

Several attack methods in the taxonomy have overlapping content. For example:
- Methods 8 (Tempering with Part Specification), 10 (Corrupting AM Process Parameters), 12 (Manipulating 3D Equipment), and 13 (Compromising 3D-Printed Parts) all involve modifying design files or parameters.
- Methods 7 (Exploitation of Supply Chain Weaknesses) and 9 (Disrupting Supply Chain Logistics) both address supply chain attacks.

The paper does not explicitly address this overlap; the distinctions appear to be based on the stage of the workflow at which the method operates (pre-manufacturing design, in-process, post-process, logistics) and the primary mechanism (cyber vs. supply chain vs. physical).

### 11.5 The relationship between "Intellectual Property Theft" as a Goal, a Target sub-vulnerability, and a Method

**Issue:** "Intellectual Property Theft" (or "IP Theft") appears simultaneously as: (a) a major Attack Goal (Goal 1: Intellectual Property Compromise, and partially Goal 3: Data Breach and Manipulation), (b) a sub-vulnerability listed under multiple attack targets (3D Design and Part Specification, IPR, CAD Model Integrity, STL Phase Integrity, Tool-Path Execution, Final Manufactured Part, AM Equipment, Export-Controlled Parts, Internally Restricted Parts), and (c) a standalone Attack Method (Method 5: Intellectual Property Theft).

**Evidence available:** This tri-level appearance is a structural feature of the paper's taxonomy. The paper treats "Intellectual Property Theft" as: a goal (the desired outcome), a target vulnerability (what the attacker exploits), and an active method (how they steal it). The paper does not explain or resolve this apparent circularity.

**Most likely reading:** This reflects the taxonomy's breadth rather than a logical error. At the Goal level, IP compromise is what the attacker wants to achieve. At the Target level, IP-containing assets are what can be exploited. At the Method level, IP theft refers to the operational technique (accessing and exfiltrating design files, trade secrets, CAD files). A reader should understand these as different aspects of the same threat at different levels of analysis.
