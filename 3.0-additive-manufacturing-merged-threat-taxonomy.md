## Purpose

This document defines a **merged threat taxonomy for additive manufacturing (AM)** by combining the two source taxonomies below into one coherent structure:

1. **Yampolskiy et al. (2018)**, *Security of additive manufacturing: Attack taxonomy and survey*
2. **Kumar et al. (2025)**, *Security of cyber-physical Additive Manufacturing supply chain: Survey, attack taxonomy and solutions*

The merged taxonomy is designed to be:

- **supply-chain-wide** rather than limited to a single AM stage
- **three-dimensional** rather than purely target/method based
- **careful about duplicates**, merging only where the concepts are materially the same
- **explicit about scope differences**, preserving useful distinctions from both papers

---

## Source-model summary

### Yampolskiy et al. (2018)

Yampolskiy structures AM threats into **two major threat categories**:

- **Theft of technical data**
- **AM sabotage**

For each category, the taxonomy uses **two top-level branches**:

- **Attack targets**
- **Attack methods**

This taxonomy is especially strong on:

- detailed AM-specific sabotage logic
- distinction between **technical data theft** and **sabotage**
- detailed treatment of **geometry**, **required properties**, **process parameters**, **post-processing**, **equipment compromise**, and **environmental impact**

### Kumar et al. (2025)

Kumar structures threats across the **entire AM supply chain** using **three top-level dimensions**:

- **Attack goals**
- **Attack targets**
- **Attack methods**

This taxonomy is especially strong on:

- supply-chain-wide scope
- broader target coverage beyond printing itself
- explicit inclusion of **unauthorised production**, **restricted/export-controlled parts**, **overproduction**, **distribution/logistics**, and **broader business/supply-chain disruption**

---

## Merge decision

The merged taxonomy uses **Kumar's high-level shape** as the umbrella structure:

1. **Attack goals** = why the attacker is acting
2. **Attack targets** = what in the AM ecosystem is being attacked
3. **Attack methods** = how the attacker achieves the attack

Within that umbrella, **Yampolskiy's detailed AM-specific categories** are used to deepen and refine the target and method branches.

This means:

- **Yampolskiy's threat families** are mapped into **Kumar's attack goals**
- **Yampolskiy's targets and methods** are preserved where they add AM-specific detail
- **duplicates are normalized**, but only when they are semantically equivalent
- **non-equivalent concepts are kept separate**

## CIA Triad Labeling Convention

Each attack goal, attack target, and attack method below is labeled with one or more CIA triad tags:

- **`[C]` Confidentiality:** unauthorized access to, disclosure of, or reconstruction of AM-related information, designs, process data, or protected knowledge
- **`[I]` Integrity:** unauthorized modification, corruption, substitution, or falsification of AM data, processes, equipment, or produced parts
- **`[A]` Availability:** disruption, delay, degradation, denial, or loss of access to AM systems, equipment, production stages, or supply-chain operations

A node may carry multiple labels (for example, `[C, I]`) where it directly affects more than one security property. Labels are applied based on the direct security role of the taxonomy entry, not on worst-case speculation.

## MITRE ATT&CK Mitigation Mapping Convention

The mitigation mappings below are a **derived analytical crosswalk** from the AM attack-method taxonomy to MITRE ATT&CK mitigations. They are not presented as MITRE-authored AM-specific mappings, and they should not be read as a claim that a listed mitigation fully eliminates the attack.

The mappings aim to be exhaustive at the level of **materially relevant ATT&CK mitigations** for each AM attack method. They intentionally do not repeat every ATT&CK mitigation under every method where the connection would be merely generic or weak.

The mapping uses MITRE ATT&CK **Enterprise** and **Industrial Control Systems (ICS)** mitigations as the primary catalogs because AM environments combine enterprise IT, engineering workstations, production networks, cyber-physical equipment, and industrial safety constraints. The MITRE pages checked for this version identify ATT&CK v19 as current. MITRE ATT&CK **Mobile** mitigations are not repeated under every method; they should be added only when the AM product or workflow includes a mobile app, mobile device management surface, or mobile-controlled production interface.

Each method-level mitigation block follows this logic:

- **Primary mitigations** are the ATT&CK mitigations most directly relevant to preventing or constraining the method.
- **Supporting mitigations** are useful hardening, recovery, monitoring, or governance measures that reduce preconditions, blast radius, or recovery time.
- **AM-specific application** explains how the generic ATT&CK mitigation concept maps onto additive-manufacturing assets such as CAD files, build jobs, slicers, process recipes, feedstock, post-processing equipment, quality data, and machine-control networks.
- **Residual coverage notes** are used where ATT&CK does not provide a complete preventive answer, especially for physical tampering, metrology-based reverse engineering, process-material science issues, natural disruptions, and legal or commercial abuse.

Mitigation entries are written in a name-first format for readability:

- Read each mitigation entry as: **mitigation name**, followed by the relevant **MITRE ATT&CK catalog** and **mitigation ID** in parentheses.
- `Data Loss Prevention (Enterprise M1057; ICS M0803)` means the same mitigation concept appears in both the Enterprise and Industrial Control Systems (ICS) ATT&CK catalogs under different IDs.
- `Remote Data Storage (Enterprise M1029)` means the mitigation is drawn from only the Enterprise catalog in this mapping.
- The sub-bullet below each mitigation entry gives a short, source-based explanation of what that MITRE mitigation means.

## CRA Mapping Convention

The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) mapping below is an **analytical crosswalk** from this AM threat taxonomy to Regulation (EU) 2024/2847. It is not legal advice and it is not a claim that a product is compliant or non-compliant.

The mapping uses **Annex I** as the primary reference because Annex I contains the essential cybersecurity requirements that can be meaningfully linked to threat categories. Articles are used as **legal anchors** that make those requirements operative:

- **Article 2** and **Article 3** define the scope and key terms, including products with digital elements.
- **Article 6** states the market requirement that products with digital elements must meet Annex I Part I and that manufacturer processes must meet Annex I Part II.
- **Article 13** sets the manufacturer's design, development, production, risk-assessment, component due-diligence, vulnerability-handling, support-period, documentation, user-information, and corrective-action obligations.
- **Article 14** adds mandatory reporting for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting the security of products with digital elements.
- **Article 31** and **Annex VII** define technical-documentation expectations.
- **Article 32** and **Annex VIII** define conformity-assessment procedures.
- **Annex II** defines user information and instructions, including secure use, support, updates, foreseeable risks, and vulnerability-reporting contact information.

The CRA applicability gate is important: the Regulation applies to **products with digital elements** whose intended purpose or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection to a device or network. Therefore, an AM threat maps strongly to the CRA only where it affects a connected AM product, software, firmware, component, remote data processing solution, vulnerability-handling process, security update, or related manufacturer process. Purely physical, contractual, IP-law, export-control, or logistics threats are marked as weak, conditional, or outside the direct CRA cybersecurity requirement set.

Mapping strength labels:

- **Direct:** the threat clearly corresponds to an Annex I cybersecurity requirement where the affected AM asset is a product with digital elements or part of one.
- **Conditional:** the mapping depends on the AM implementation, for example whether the asset is digitally connected, software-controlled, firmware-based, remotely processed, or sold as a component.
- **Partial/gap:** the CRA addresses only a supporting cybersecurity aspect of the threat, not the threat as a whole.
- **Mostly outside CRA:** the threat is mainly physical, legal, commercial, export-control, or operational, and only becomes CRA-relevant if it is enabled through a product with digital elements.

Reference notation used in the CRA crosswalk:

- `Annex I.I(1)` means Annex I, Part I, point (1): risk-based appropriate cybersecurity.
- `Annex I.I(2)(a-m)` means Annex I, Part I, point (2), subpoints (a) to (m), covering product security properties.
- `Annex I.II(1-8)` means Annex I, Part II, points (1) to (8), covering vulnerability handling.
- `Annex II` means information and instructions to the user.
- `Annex VII` means technical documentation.

## Canonical merged taxonomy

# 1. Attack goals

Attack goals describe the attacker's intended outcome.

## 1.1 Intellectual property compromise / technical data theft `[C]`

The attacker seeks to obtain, reconstruct, copy, or misuse protected AM-related knowledge.

Includes:

- theft of technical data
  - Covers unauthorised acquisition of AM technical representations or process knowledge that can enable reproduction, modification, or competitive use.
- intellectual property theft
  - Targets AM IP such as unique designs, manufacturing techniques, product innovations, trade secrets, or other protected know-how.
- CAD file theft
  - Involves unauthorised access to proprietary CAD models or design files that define the product blueprint.
- design theft
  - Covers acquisition of protected design information that can be used to duplicate, modify, or understand an AM object.
- process-knowledge theft
  - Targets manufacturing procedures, printing settings, material specifications, or production techniques that reveal how the part is made.
- trade secret theft
  - Covers confidential AM knowledge such as material compositions, post-processing methods, or unique manufacturing processes.
- counterfeiting and unlicensed reproduction
  - Uses protected or copied AM knowledge to produce fake products or replicas without the required rights or permissions.
- reverse-engineered reproduction
  - Recreates AM designs or manufacturing information from physical objects, scans, side channels, or equipment analysis rather than direct file access.
- industrial espionage
  - Involves infiltrating or compromising AM organisations to obtain proprietary information for competitive or financial advantage.
- unauthorised reproduction based on stolen or reconstructed knowledge
  - Produces AM parts from stolen or reverse-engineered knowledge without authorisation from the legitimate rights holder.

## 1.2 Unauthorised production / counterfeit / restricted-part production `[I]`

The attacker seeks to produce parts that are not authorised by the legitimate owner, regulator, or internal control regime.

Includes:

- counterfeit production
  - Produces parts that imitate genuine components and may deceive buyers or fail to meet expected quality and safety standards.
- unlicensed reproduction
  - Replicates proprietary AM parts or designs without the rights, licences, or permissions of the legitimate owner.
- grey-market production
  - Creates AM parts outside authorised production and distribution channels, often to supply unofficial markets.
- grey-market operations
  - Runs unauthorised AM production or sales activity without consent from original producers or authorised distributors.
- unauthorised overproduction
  - Produces more parts than the legitimate order, approval, or production authorisation allows.
- production of export-controlled parts
  - Uses AM to produce parts or technologies whose manufacture or transfer is restricted by export-control rules.
- production of internally restricted parts
  - Produces parts that an organisation or facility restricts because of safety, security, legal, regulatory, or policy concerns.
- evasion of supply-chain controls
  - Uses decentralised or localised AM production to avoid permitted manufacturing routes and make source tracing harder.
- bypassing regulatory or contractual constraints
  - Produces parts outside required licences, certifications, contractual limits, or other authorisation controls.
- bypassing regulatory compliance
  - Manufactures AM components without required compliance steps, especially in regulated sectors where safety and legal risks follow.

## 1.3 Data breach and data manipulation `[C, I]`

The attacker seeks unauthorised access to AM-related data or seeks to alter that data to influence production, inspection, quality, or downstream use.

Includes:

- data infiltration
  - Breaks into AM systems to access valuable data such as CAD files, STL models, slicing settings, G-Code, or confidential design knowledge.
- data exfiltration
  - Removes AM data from compromised systems, potentially exposing sensitive information over time.
- design and process information access
  - Accesses design parameters, material specifications, or printing settings to learn about quality, performance, or distinctive product features.
- manipulation of design files
  - Changes AM design files to influence the geometry, integrity, safety, or functionality of the printed object.
- manipulation of process data
  - Alters AM process-related data such as printing settings, slicing parameters, material information, or production instructions.
- manipulation of quality-control data
  - Changes inspection, testing, or acceptance data so defective or manipulated parts may appear compliant.
- manipulation of sensor readings
  - Falsifies process or environmental readings used for monitoring, control, quality assurance, or safety decisions.
- manipulation of machine instructions
  - Alters G-Code, tool paths, or equivalent machine instructions that determine how the printer executes the build.
- tampering with AM-related data or control representations
  - Broadly covers unauthorised changes to AM digital artefacts or control data that influence production or downstream use.

## 1.4 Sabotage / integrity degradation `[I, A]`

The attacker seeks to reduce the integrity, reliability, safety, or functional fitness of the AM process or the resulting object.

Includes:

- reduction of mechanical strength
  - Degrades the part below required mechanical tolerance so it may fail under normal operational conditions.
- reduction of fatigue life
  - Shortens the part's useful lifetime so failure can occur prematurely during repeated or long-term use.
- introduction of hidden defects
  - Inserts flaws such as internal weaknesses, voids, or defects that may remain undetected during quality control.
- introducing weaknesses
  - Creates vulnerabilities in the part or process that reduce durability, reliability, or expected performance.
- material/property degradation
  - Alters material characteristics or part properties so the final object no longer meets its required function.
- tampering with material properties
  - Manipulates material composition, process parameters, or related conditions to change the properties of the produced part.
- contamination
  - Introduces unwanted material, foreign objects, or NBC contamination into feedstock, equipment, or parts.
- compromising part integrity
  - Undermines the final part's fit, form, function, structure, or conformance to its authorised specification.
- equipment damage
  - Harms AM mechanical, electrical, cyber, or safety-critical equipment, causing wear, malfunction, or irreparable damage.
- environmental damage
  - Causes harm beyond the part itself, including effects on nearby equipment, facility systems, customers, or deployed environments.
- disrupting production processes
  - Interferes with printers, software systems, network connectivity, or production steps to cause delays, waste, or degraded output.
- targeting critical components
  - Focuses sabotage on safety-critical or mission-critical parts where failure can cause system failures or accidents.
- concealing defects
  - Makes compromised parts appear visually or procedurally acceptable despite hidden weaknesses or structural problems.
- quality degradation that leads to unsafe or substandard output
  - Produces parts whose integrity, functionality, or safety is reduced below expected quality or performance standards.

## 1.5 Availability disruption / denial of service `[A]`

The attacker seeks to interrupt, delay, disable, overload, or render unavailable AM operations or supply-chain flows.

Includes:

- denial of service
  - Disrupts service availability so AM systems, equipment, production stages, or supply-chain operations cannot function as needed.
- ransomware-driven lockout
  - Locks AM control systems or related infrastructure, delaying production and creating operational or financial losses.
- production stoppage
  - Halts or interrupts AM production, causing downtime, delayed output, material waste, or recovery costs.
- logistics disruption
  - Disrupts transportation, routing, delivery, or distribution flows that AM supply-chain continuity depends on.
- equipment unavailability
  - Makes AM printers, post-processing equipment, control systems, or supporting devices unavailable for production.
- power-related disruption
  - Interrupts or destabilises power needed for AM temperature control, environmental stability, equipment operation, or safe completion of builds.
- natural disasters
  - Represents exploited disaster conditions such as floods, earthquakes, hurricanes, or wildfires that can damage AM facilities or infrastructure.
- power outages
  - Interrupt printing and supporting environmental conditions, potentially causing incomplete builds, material waste, or equipment damage.
- material contamination
  - Covers disruption conditions where AM materials absorb moisture, particulates, or other contaminants that affect material properties or part quality.
- temperature and humidity fluctuations
  - Affect AM material behaviour and process consistency, especially where powders or thermal conditions are sensitive.
- disruption of supply-chain continuity
  - Breaks the dependable flow of materials, data, equipment, parts, or services needed to sustain AM operations.

# 2. Attack targets

Attack targets describe the assets, stages, artefacts, systems, environments, or protected objects that can be attacked.

To make the merged taxonomy usable, the targets are grouped by **AM lifecycle zone**.

## 2.1 Design and specification assets `[C, I]`

Targets in this family are the digital and specification artefacts that define the intended 3D object, including geometry, material characteristics, required properties, design representations, and machine-ready instructions.

### 2.1.1 Part specification `[C, I]`

The part specification captures the geometry, properties, appearance, material choices, and interface details needed to define what the AM part should be and how it should perform.

Merged from Yampolskiy's detailed theft/sabotage treatment and Kumar's broader design/specification treatment.

Includes:

- **Part geometry**
  - external shape
  - internal cavities
  - dimensions
  - tolerances
  - surface finish requirements
  - mass / center-of-mass relevant geometry
- **Required properties**
  - mechanical properties
  - thermal properties
  - chemical properties
  - manufacturing properties
  - electrical or other functional properties where relevant
- **Colour / texture / appearance specifications**
- **Material specification**
- **Assembly/interface features**
- **Feature addition/removal opportunities**

### 2.1.2 CAD model integrity `[C, I]`

The CAD model is the native design representation of the 3D object and may contain shape, dimensions, features, design intent, revisions, constraints, and proprietary product knowledge.

Includes:

- CAD files
- model revisions
- embedded design intent
- metadata and constraints inside CAD tooling

### 2.1.3 STL / AMF / 3MF / intermediate model integrity `[C, I]`

The intermediate model is the converted downstream representation of the design, such as STL surface geometry or richer AMF/3MF object data, used before slicing and machine execution.

Includes:

- STL integrity
- mesh integrity
- converted geometry integrity
- format-specific object representation

### 2.1.4 Tool-path / slicing output / machine-instruction integrity `[C, I]`

Tool paths, slicing outputs, and machine instructions are the executable manufacturing representations that translate the design into printer movements, layer behavior, process commands, and command ordering.

Includes:

- slicing parameters
- generated tool-paths
- G-code or equivalent control representations
- proprietary machine instructions
- timing/order of manufacturing commands

---

## 2.2 Process knowledge and production recipe assets `[C, I, A]`

Targets in this family are the process recipes, live production states, post-processing instructions, sensor data, and dependent production knowledge that determine how AM material is formed, treated, inspected, and accepted.

### 2.2.1 Manufacturing process specification `[C, I]`

The manufacturing process specification defines the AM process category and parameters, such as power, speed, hatch spacing, build direction, supports, atmosphere, feedstock settings, and machine-specific know-how that influence microstructure and properties.

A major Yampolskiy target preserved as a first-class node.

Includes:

- scan strategy
- power / speed / hatch spacing
- layer parameters
- build direction
- support strategy
- atmosphere/environment settings
- feedstock-related process settings
- machine-specific process know-how
- **Power Bed Fusion (PBF)**
  - build parameters
  - support structures
  - build direction
  - feedstock
  - environment
  - build temperature
- **Direct Energy Deposition (DED)**
  - environment
- additional AM process categories where the source taxonomy used open-ended ellipsis nodes

### 2.2.2 Manufacturing process execution `[I, A]`

Manufacturing process execution is the live build-stage operation where control commands, sequencing, parameter changes, machine state, material handling, and system access affect the printed output.

Includes:

- process control commands
- execution sequencing
- live parameter control
- real-time machine behavior
- machine state transitions

### 2.2.3 Post-processing specification and execution `[C, I, A]`

Post-processing covers the required post-build treatments and their execution, including heat treatment, HIPing, finish machining, surface treatment, inspection, and related settings that affect material properties, fit, surface quality, and acceptance.

Merged from Yampolskiy's **post-processing specification** and Kumar's **post-processing stage**.

Includes:

- heat-treatment specification
- hot isostatic pressing specification
- finish machining specification
- coating/surface-treatment specification
- inspection/test configuration used during post-processing
- live execution of post-processing steps
- **Heat treatment**
  - HIPing
  - annealing
- **Finish machining**
  - EDM
  - surface finish
- surface contamination
- structural modifications
- introduction of hidden components
- substitution of post-processing materials
- data alteration during post-processing
- sabotage of post-processing equipment

### 2.2.4 Sensor information and quality-control data `[C, I, A]`

Sensor information and quality-control data are monitoring, inspection, and feedback artefacts used to estimate process state, guide control loops, verify conformance, or decide whether a part should be accepted.

Pulled from Yampolskiy's process-control discussion and retained as a practical target class.

Includes:

- in-situ monitoring data
- IR thermography data
- environmental sensor data
- quality-control measurements
- inspection/test results
- closed-loop control feedback

### 2.2.5 Indirect manufacturing dependencies `[C, I, A]`

Indirect manufacturing dependencies are AM-produced or third-party assets, such as tooling, molds, supporting infrastructure, suppliers, software, or power dependencies, whose compromise can affect the final manufactured outcome.

Merged from both papers.

Includes:

- molds, tools, jigs, and intermediate AM-produced manufacturing aids
- external dependencies whose compromise affects the final AM outcome
- dependent processes needed to produce the eventual final part
- tooling
- molds
- third-party supplier dependencies
- cyber or power infrastructure dependencies
- insider and social-engineering exposure in dependent processes

## 2.3 Supply-chain assets and dependencies `[C, I, A]`

Targets in this family are the materials, parts, equipment, software, services, logistics flows, and hand-off points that connect AM production to upstream suppliers and downstream distribution.

### 2.3.1 Feedstock / source materials `[I, A]`

Feedstock and source materials are the powders, filaments, resins, wires, recycled streams, and material characteristics whose chemistry, contamination, size distribution, moisture, or oxygen exposure can affect part quality and process safety.

A key Yampolskiy supply-chain target retained explicitly.

Includes:

- powders
- filaments
- resins
- wire feedstock
- recycled/reblended material streams
- material chemistry, size distribution, moisture, oxygen exposure, contaminants
- chemical composition
- powder characteristics
- recycling
- sieving
- blending properties
- NBC contamination

### 2.3.2 Replacement parts and upgrades `[I, A]`

Replacement parts and upgrades are maintenance, repair, software, firmware, mechanical, electrical, and electronic components introduced after deployment that can affect machine control, process behavior, or equipment reliability.

Includes:

- replacement mechanical parts
- electrical parts
- firmware updates
- software updates
- maintenance components
- third-party upgrades
- software / firmware replacement parts
- mechanical replacement parts
- electrical replacement parts
- electronic replacement parts

### 2.3.3 AM equipment delivered through the supply chain `[C, I, A]`

This target covers AM machines and related equipment before, during, or after delivery, where printers, post-processing devices, or quality-control devices may arrive from suppliers or intermediaries in a compromised state.

Includes:

- 3D printers
- post-processing equipment
- quality-control devices
- compromised machines delivered from suppliers or intermediaries

### 2.3.4 Third-party software, firmware, and service dependencies `[C, I, A]`

Third-party software, firmware, and services are external digital dependencies in the AM tool chain, including slicers, machine-control software, process modeling, quality software, cloud collaboration, and remote maintenance links.

Expanded from Kumar's supply-chain scope and Yampolskiy's cyber infrastructure notion.

Includes:

- OEM firmware
- slicers
- machine-control software
- process-modeling software
- quality software
- cloud platforms
- collaboration systems
- networked maintenance connections
- remote support links

### 2.3.5 Logistics and distribution chain `[C, I, A]`

The logistics and distribution chain covers movement, storage, tracking, packaging, and hand-off of design data, feedstock, spare parts, equipment, and finished parts across AM supply-chain actors.

Explicitly retained from Kumar.

Includes:

- movement of design data
- movement of feedstock
- movement of spare parts
- movement of finished parts
- packaging, storage, and hand-off points
- shipment tracking systems

### 2.3.6 Manufactured objects in transit or return flows `[I, A]`

Manufactured objects in transit or return flows are finished or returned parts that may be replaced, contaminated, tampered with, or otherwise compromised during shipping, warehousing, repair, recycling, or return handling.

Merged from Yampolskiy's "manufactured object" supply-chain target and Kumar's downstream delivery/return context.

Includes:

- parts in shipping
- parts in warehousing
- parts returned for repair/recycling
- counterfeit replacement during delivery/return

## 2.4 Physical production system assets `[C, I, A]`

Targets in this family are the physical AM outputs, equipment, production surroundings, and downstream environments that can suffer confidentiality, integrity, availability, safety, or functional consequences.

### 2.4.1 Final manufactured part `[C, I]`

The final manufactured part is the central sabotage target because AM creates the material and the part together, so fit, form, properties, strength, fatigue life, microstructure, and contamination can all affect function and safety.

Includes:

- **Fit and form**
  - shape and dimensions
  - mass
  - center of mass
- **Function**
  - physical properties
  - chemical properties
  - thermal properties
  - electrical properties
  - magnetic properties
  - optical properties
  - mechanical properties
- **Mechanical strength**
- **Fatigue life**
- **Microstructure**
- **Hidden flaws / voids / microfractures**
- **Material composition and internal integrity**
- **Contamination / embedded objects / hidden components**

### 2.4.2 AM equipment `[C, I, A]`

AM equipment includes printers, post-processing devices, quality-control devices, mechanical and electrical components, cyber infrastructure, networks, safety subsystems, and machine elements whose compromise can disrupt production or alter output.

Merged from both papers, keeping Yampolskiy's detailed split.

Includes:

- **Mechanical components**
  - increased wear
  - irreparable damage
- **Electrical components**
- **Cyber infrastructure**
  - software
  - firmware
  - hardware
  - networks
  - availability
  - integrity
  - timing
- **Safety-critical subsystems**
- **Inspection / post-processing machines**
- explosion / fire

### 2.4.3 AM production environment `[I, A]`

The AM production environment is the physical surrounding in which printing occurs, including temperature, humidity, contamination, ventilation, vibration, power stability, physical access, and emergency conditions that affect process quality and availability.

Merged from Yampolskiy's environment focus and Kumar's production-environment target.

Includes:

- temperature
- humidity
- contaminants/dust
- ventilation/fumes
- vibration/noise
- power-supply stability
- physical access security
- nearby equipment and surrounding facility conditions
- emergency preparedness

### 2.4.4 Downstream environment affected by deployed part `[I, A]`

The downstream environment is the customer, host system, nearby equipment, facility, or mission context that can be harmed when a compromised AM machine or deployed part causes physical, cyber-physical, functional, or stealth-related effects.

A Yampolskiy-style environmental target retained because it captures downstream physical harm.

Includes:

- host system using the sabotaged part
- nearby equipment harmed by machine malfunction
- customer environment exposed to contaminated or compromised parts
- mission/system environment impacted by stealth-signature or functional degradation
- machine shop tools
  - disruption

# 4. Cross-cutting target-to-method relationships

The merged taxonomy should be used with the following logic:

- **Goals** explain the attacker's end state
- **Targets** identify where in the AM ecosystem the attack lands
- **Methods** identify how the attack is carried out

A single goal can be pursued through multiple targets and methods.
A single target can support multiple goals.
A single method can impact multiple targets.

Examples:

- **IP compromise** may target:
  - part specification
  - CAD model integrity
  - manufacturing process specification
  - post-processing specification
  - tool-path integrity
  - machine-resident process knowledge

- **Sabotage** may target:
  - final manufactured part
  - manufacturing process execution
  - post-processing execution
  - feedstock
  - AM equipment
  - production environment

- **Availability disruption** may target:
  - AM equipment
  - logistics chain
  - third-party software/firmware services
  - power supply stability
  - production scheduling/throughput

# 5. CRA Annex I Crosswalk

This section links the full canonical AM threat taxonomy to the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, using the attached CRA text as the source. The crosswalk is intentionally requirements-first: each taxonomy node is linked primarily to **Annex I** and then to the relevant CRA articles that make those requirements legally operative.

The crosswalk covers every canonical node in sections **1. Attack goals**, **2. Attack targets**, and **3. Attack methods**. The cross-cutting relationships in section 4 are not mapped separately because they are combinations of those already mapped nodes.

## 5.1 CRA scope and reference key

The CRA mapping should always be read through the following scope gate:

- The CRA applies to products with digital elements made available on the market where the intended purpose or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection to a device or network, as set out in Article 2(1).
- Article 3(1) defines a product with digital elements as a software or hardware product and its remote data processing solutions, including software or hardware components placed on the market separately.
- Article 6 links market availability to Annex I: products must meet Annex I Part I, and manufacturer processes must meet Annex I Part II.
- Article 13 requires manufacturers to perform cybersecurity risk assessment, account for the results across planning, design, development, production, delivery, and maintenance, and document that assessment in the technical documentation.
- Threats that concern purely physical materials, business authorization, export control, counterfeiting as a legal act, or general logistics are not directly solved by the CRA unless they are enabled by or affect a product with digital elements.

## 5.5 CRA coverage summary

| CRA coverage level | Threat-taxonomy areas | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Strong CRA coverage | Data breach/manipulation; CAD/STL/toolpath tampering; process-control manipulation; cyber intrusion on AM equipment; third-party software/firmware/service compromise; insecure updates; known exploitable vulnerabilities; DoS against product functions | These map directly to Annex I.I(2)(a), (c), (d), (e), (f), (h), (j), (k), (l), and Annex I.II(1)-(8). |
| Conditional CRA coverage | AM equipment, post-processing systems, manufactured parts, replacement parts, embedded components, remote services, sensor/QC data, indirect manufacturing dependencies | These are CRA-relevant when they are products with digital elements, components, remote data processing solutions, or connected systems within Article 2(1) and Article 3(1). |
| Partial CRA coverage | Feedstock compromise; logistics disruption; manufactured-object compromise in transit; AM production environment; downstream environment affected by a deployed part | The CRA may cover digital monitoring, availability, instructions, product impact, or risk assessment, but not the entire physical or operational threat. |
| Mostly outside direct CRA coverage | Counterfeiting as a legal/commercial act; unlicensed reproduction as an IP violation; overproduction as a contractual or business-control issue; export-controlled part production; internally restricted part policy; natural disasters; labour strikes; geopolitical instability; purely physical sabotage | These threats can become CRA-relevant only if they are enabled by or affect a product with digital elements. The CRA is not a general AM governance, export-control, IP, logistics, material-quality, or physical-security regulation. |

The useful research conclusion is therefore narrow but strong: the CRA can be linked rigorously to the **digital cyber-resilience layer** of AM threats, especially data, commands, software, firmware, connected equipment, vulnerabilities, updates, and component dependencies. It only partially covers the broader AM supply-chain threat surface.

# 6. What was merged, and what was kept distinct

## 6.1 Items merged as duplicates or near-duplicates

These were merged because they describe essentially the same attack idea at different abstraction levels.

- **Theft** + **Intellectual property theft**
- **Protection removal** + **Bypassing digital and physical protection**
- **3D part specification manipulation** + **Tempering with part specification**
- **AM manufacturing process manipulation** + **Compromising AM process parameters**
- **Post-processing manipulation** + **Tempering with post-processing procedures**
- **Compromised AM equipment** + **Cyber intrusion / physical sabotage / manipulating 3D equipment**
- **Compromised part** + **Compromising 3D-printed parts**
- **Part specification** + **3D design and part specification** (while preserving Yampolskiy's internal substructure)
- **Post-processing specification** + **Post-processing stage**
- **Environment** + **AM production environment** (while preserving Yampolskiy's downstream-environment distinction)

## 6.2 Items intentionally kept distinct

These were **not** collapsed because the distinction is analytically useful.

- **Intellectual property compromise** vs **Unauthorised production**
  - stealing knowledge is not the same as producing illegal parts
- **Manufacturing process specification** vs **Manufacturing process execution**
  - recipe knowledge is different from live process control
- **CAD integrity** vs **STL/intermediate integrity** vs **Tool-path integrity**
  - they are distinct control points in the AM digital chain
- **Feedstock compromise** vs **Equipment compromise**
  - both are supply-chain vectors but attack different physical dependencies
- **Final manufactured part** vs **Downstream environment affected by the part**
  - the part itself and the harm it later causes are not the same target surface
- **Unauthorised overproduction** vs **Counterfeit/restricted-part production**
  - overproduction is an operational/business-control issue that can occur even without a new design being introduced

# 7. Elements preserved as secondary annotations rather than top-level nodes

Some source concepts are important, but they fit better as **annotations** than as top-level merged dimensions.

## 7.1 Yampolskiy's first-order causal effects

Yampolskiy includes **1st order causal effects** under the sabotage target side. In this merged taxonomy, these are treated as **outcomes/consequences**, not primary targets.

Typical examples:

- 1st order causal effects affecting the manufacturing site
  - manufacturing-site availability
- 1st order causal effects affecting equipment
  - equipment availability
  - equipment life time
- 1st order causal effects affecting manufactured-part outcomes
  - average material use
  - average time per part
  - average part acceptance
  - breakage conditions
- equipment wear or damage
- failed quality acceptance
- damage to the system using the sabotaged part
- downstream contamination or physical harm

## 7.2 Kumar's exploited opportunities

Kumar distinguishes between **attacker goals** and **exploited opportunities**. In this merged taxonomy, exploited opportunities are preserved as **contextual exploit conditions**, not primary goals.

Examples:

- attacker goals
- cyber goals
- physical goals
- natural disruptions
- man-made disruptions / man-made events
- emergency or instability windows
- economic/market instability
- natural disasters
- power outages
- material contamination
- temperature and humidity fluctuations
- labour strikes
- geopolitical instability

This keeps the core taxonomy cleaner while preserving the operational insight.

# 8. Recommended short-form version of the merged taxonomy

For practical use, the taxonomy can be remembered as:

## Goals

- IP compromise / technical data theft `[C]`
- unauthorised production `[I]`
- data breach/manipulation `[C, I]`
- sabotage `[I, A]`
- availability disruption `[A]`

## Targets

- design/specification assets `[C, I]`
- process and recipe assets `[C, I, A]`
- supply-chain assets and dependencies `[C, I, A]`
- physical production system assets `[C, I, A]`
- rights/restricted-object targets `[C, I]`

## Methods

- acquisition/exfiltration `[C, I]`
- protection bypass / unauthorised reproduction `[C, I]`
- design/specification tampering `[I]`
- process tampering `[I, A]`
- post-processing tampering `[I]`
- supply-chain compromise `[C, I, A]`
- equipment compromise `[C, I, A]`
- part compromise `[I, A]`
- contextual disruption exploitation `[I, A]`

## Mitigation mapping

Each concrete attack method carries a derived MITRE ATT&CK mitigation block:

- **Primary mitigations:** the ATT&CK mitigation categories most directly relevant to constraining the method
- **Supporting mitigations:** hardening, detection, recovery, governance, or resilience controls that reduce prerequisites or impact
- **AM-specific application:** how the mitigation categories apply to AM assets, workflows, equipment, or supply-chain stages
- **Residual coverage note:** where ATT&CK is incomplete or only indirectly useful, especially for physical, material, logistics, legal, or emergency-context risks

The short version is: **goals explain why, targets explain where, methods explain how, and the MITRE mapping explains what defensive control families are relevant.**

## CRA mapping

The CRA crosswalk should be read as: **taxonomy node -> Annex I cybersecurity requirement -> legal article anchor -> coverage strength**.

- Annex I is the primary mapping target.
- Articles 6 and 13 are the most common legal anchors.
- Article 14 is relevant when the threat involves an actively exploited vulnerability or severe incident affecting product security.
- Article 31 and Annex VII are relevant when the threat needs to be reflected in technical documentation, risk assessment, tests, architecture descriptions, vulnerability-handling processes, or SBOM-related evidence.
- Threats involving materials, logistics, counterfeiting, export control, overproduction, or physical sabotage are only partly covered unless they are enabled by or affect a product with digital elements.

# 9. Suggested canonical tree notation

The tree remains goal-target-method oriented. The MITRE ATT&CK content is attached at the **concrete method leaf** level rather than as a fourth top-level taxonomy branch, because mitigations respond to how an attack is carried out. The CRA crosswalk is attached across the **goal**, **target**, and **method** nodes because the CRA is used as a regulatory-requirements traceability layer rather than as a mitigation taxonomy.

## Final note

This merged taxonomy should be treated as a **synthesized analytical model**, not as a verbatim reproduction of either source paper.

It preserves:

- **Kumar's supply-chain-wide scope and goal-target-method structure**
- **Yampolskiy's AM-specific detail on part specification, process manipulation, sabotage, equipment compromise, and environment**

The result is a more complete AM threat taxonomy for research, threat modeling, and compliance-oriented security analysis. It contains AM threat goals, targets, methods, and method-level mitigation mappings, not ready-made product-specific scenarios. Product-specific risk assessment items are created later by applying those taxonomy entries to the selected product, version, remote data processing solution scope, environment, and assets.

Where this taxonomy is operationalised inside the CRA compliance SaaS, it should be persisted as structured reference data in `PostgreSQL` via `Prisma`, with `Neon` as a reasonable managed Postgres option, so the taxonomy's categories, patterns, and mappings can be queried, reused, and extended across SaaS workflows.
