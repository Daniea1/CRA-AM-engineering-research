## 2. Purpose of the taxonomy

The authors propose two attack taxonomies for Additive Manufacturing (AM) security — one for **theft of technical data** (also called IP violation) and one for **AM sabotage**. The stated purpose is to support "systematization of knowledge" about attacks on or with AM, following the tradition in security research of developing taxonomies that enumerate aspects of attacks. The authors argue that existing research on AM vulnerabilities has reached sufficient maturity to justify formalizing these taxonomies.

Each taxonomy serves two audiences simultaneously:
1. Researchers new to AM Security, who need a structured introduction to the threat landscape.
2. Active researchers, who need a comprehensive, structured reference to situate existing and new work.

For each threat category, the taxonomies enumerate:
- **Attack targets** — what an adversary aims to compromise or steal.
- **Attack methods** — how the adversary achieves the attack.
- **Which methods can achieve which targets** — expressed as correlation/association matrices.

The taxonomies are explicitly scoped to aspects covered by **relevant natural science domains** (e.g., cybersecurity, materials science, mechanical engineering). They are not intended to cover socioeconomic, political, or legal dimensions.

## 3. Organizing principles used by the authors

### 3.1 The underlying framework

The authors ground both taxonomies in a prior framework for analysis of attacks on or with AM (Yampolskiy et al. [29]). According to this framework, attacks involve the following sequential chain of concepts:

```
Attack Vectors
    → Compromised Element(s)
        → Manipulations
            (+ AM equipment type, source materials, application area)
                → Effects
                    → [intersection with adversarial goals]
                        → Attack Targets (= Threats)
                            → Adversarial Goals & Objectives
```

- **Attack vectors:** The means by which an adversary gains access (e.g., phishing email, network intrusion).
- **Compromised elements:** The parts of the AM workflow that are under adversarial control.
- **Manipulations:** The concrete actions an adversary performs on compromised elements.
- **Effects:** The outcomes of those manipulations, conditioned on AM equipment type, source materials, and object application area.
- **Attack targets (threats):** The intersection of achievable effects and adversarial goals. Only effects that serve the adversary's goals count as attack targets.
- **Adversarial goals & objectives:** The ultimate aims of the adversary; objectives are intermediate "stepping stones."

### 3.2 Adjustments made for taxonomy definition

Because the same manipulation can be introduced through different compromised elements (e.g., a geometry change can be introduced via a compromised Controller PC, a compromised internal network, or compromised firmware), the authors introduce the following conceptual mappings to provide a cleaner taxonomy:

- **Attack methods** are defined as **semantically identical manipulations**, regardless of which compromised element introduces them or what their syntactic representation is. This collapses multiple specific implementation paths into one conceptual category.
- **Attack targets** are defined as **effects that directly correspond to adversarial goals**, eliminating effects that are merely intermediate or incidental.

### 3.3 Top-level structure of each taxonomy

For each of the two threat categories, the taxonomy has exactly **two top-level branches**:
1. **Attack targets** — organized by what is being compromised.
2. **Attack methods** — organized by how the compromise is achieved.

These two branches are then linked by a **correlation matrix** showing which methods can achieve which targets. The paper uses two symbols in those matrices:
- **Filled circle (●):** Achievable — the method can fully achieve the target.
- **Open circle (○):** Partly/conditionally achievable — the method can only partially or conditionally achieve the target.

### 3.4 Major threat categories considered

The paper identifies the following major AM security threat categories (Fig. 4):

1. **Theft of technical data** (= IP violation) — *Focus of this paper*
2. **AM sabotage** — *Focus of this paper*
3. **Illegal part manufacturing** — explicitly **excluded** from this paper's scope:
   - Export-controlled parts (e.g., firearms)
   - Internationally prohibited parts (e.g., explosive device components)
   - Reason for exclusion: No security-related technical differences exist between illegal manufacturing using AM or using traditional CNC machines; only legal aspects have been discussed in peer-reviewed literature.

### 3.5 CIA triad mapping

- **Theft of technical data** → Confidentiality concern
- **AM sabotage (data/process alteration)** → Integrity concern
- **AM sabotage (process impairment/denial)** → Availability concern

### 3.6 Taxonomy scope constraint

> "In our proposal (presented in Sections 4 and 5), we focus exclusively on aspects covered by relevant natural science domains."

This means the taxonomy does not encompass: legal aspects, socioeconomic motivations, organizational countermeasures, or policy dimensions — even though the paper surveys those topics separately in Section 6.

### 3.7 Comparison to other AM security taxonomies

The authors explicitly state their taxonomy differs from contemporaneous proposals (Pan et al. [53]; Wu et al. [54]; Gupta et al. [55]; Yampolskiy et al. [29]) in three respects:
- **Narrower scope:** Focuses only on natural-science aspects.
- **Lower abstraction level:** Provides significantly greater depth than other proposals.
- **Different organizing principles:** Groups semantically identical manipulations from different compromised elements into a single attack method (rather than listing each compromised element separately).

### 3.8 CIA Triad Labeling Convention

Each attack method in this document is labeled with one or more CIA triad tags indicating which security properties it threatens:

- **`[C]` — Confidentiality:** The method enables unauthorized access to or disclosure of sensitive information (e.g., IP, design files, process specifications).
- **`[I]` — Integrity:** The method enables unauthorized modification or corruption of data, files, or physical outputs (e.g., altering design geometry, injecting defects).
- **`[A]` — Availability:** The method disrupts or denies access to systems, equipment, or production processes (e.g., ransomware, denial of service, physical destruction).

A method may carry multiple labels (e.g., `[C, I]`) where it threatens more than one property. Labels are applied based on the primary and secondary effects described in the taxonomy, not worst-case speculation.

### 3.9 CRA Annex Labeling Convention

Each attack method in this document is labeled with one or more CRA Annex tags indicating which specific provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act (EU Regulation 2024/2847) are most directly relevant to preventing, detecting, or mitigating that attack. Labels reference the exact Annex, Part, and paragraph or sub-paragraph.

**Annex I, Part I — Essential Cybersecurity Requirements (Product Properties)**

Products with digital elements shall be designed, developed, and produced to ensure an appropriate level of cybersecurity. The following sub-provisions are used as labels:

- **`[AI.I §1(a)]`** — Products shall be delivered without any known exploitable vulnerabilities.
- **`[AI.I §1(b)]`** — Products shall be delivered with a secure by default configuration, including the ability to reset to original state.
- **`[AI.I §1(c)]`** — Products shall ensure protection from unauthorised access by appropriate control mechanisms, including authentication, identity, or access management systems.
- **`[AI.I §1(d)]`** — Products shall protect the confidentiality of stored, transmitted, or otherwise processed data (personal or other), such as by encrypting relevant data at rest or in transit.
- **`[AI.I §1(e)]`** — Products shall protect the integrity of stored, transmitted, or otherwise processed data, commands, programs, and configuration against unauthorised manipulation or modification, and shall report on corruptions.
- **`[AI.I §1(f)]`** — Products shall process only data that is adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the intended use (data minimisation).
- **`[AI.I §1(g)]`** — Products shall protect the availability of essential functions, including resilience against and mitigation of denial of service attacks.
- **`[AI.I §1(h)]`** — Products shall minimise their own negative impact on the availability of services provided by other devices or networks.
- **`[AI.I §1(i)]`** — Products shall be designed, developed, and produced to limit attack surfaces, including external interfaces.
- **`[AI.I §1(j)]`** — Products shall be designed, developed, and produced to reduce the impact of an incident using appropriate exploitation mitigation mechanisms and techniques.
- **`[AI.I §1(k)]`** — Products shall provide security-related information by recording and/or monitoring relevant internal activity, including access to or modification of data, services, or functions.
- **`[AI.I §1(l)]`** — Products shall ensure that vulnerabilities can be addressed through security updates, including, where applicable, through automatic updates and notification of available updates to users.

**Annex I, Part II — Vulnerability Handling Requirements**

Manufacturers of products with digital elements shall meet the following obligations. The following sub-provisions are used as labels:

- **`[AI.II §1]`** — Manufacturers shall identify and document vulnerabilities and components in the product, including by drawing up a software bill of materials (SBOM) in a commonly used, machine-readable format.
- **`[AI.II §2]`** — Manufacturers shall address and remediate vulnerabilities without delay, including by providing security updates; where technically feasible, security updates shall be provided separately from functionality updates.
- **`[AI.II §3]`** — Manufacturers shall apply effective and regular testing and reviews of product security.
- **`[AI.II §4]`** — Once a security update is available, manufacturers shall disclose information about fixed vulnerabilities, including descriptions, affected products, impacts, severity, and remediation guidance.
- **`[AI.II §5]`** — Manufacturers shall put in place and enforce a policy on coordinated vulnerability disclosure.
- **`[AI.II §6]`** — Manufacturers shall take measures to facilitate sharing of information about potential vulnerabilities in their product and in third-party components used in it.
- **`[AI.II §7]`** — Manufacturers shall provide mechanisms to securely distribute updates for products with digital elements to ensure timely remediation of exploitable vulnerabilities.
- **`[AI.II §8]`** — Manufacturers shall ensure that security patches or updates are disseminated without delay and, unless justified, free of charge — including, where technically feasible, via automatic updates.

**Annex II — Information and Instructions to Users**

- **`[AII]`** — Annex II sets out the information and instructions that must accompany products with digital elements. This label applies where an attack succeeds in part because end-users lack the mandatory security guidance, configuration instructions, or risk information that Annex II requires the manufacturer to provide.

**Annex III — Important Products with Digital Elements**

- **`[AIII-I]`** — The attack targets a Class I important product (e.g., identity management software, password managers, network monitoring tools, routers, switches, microbrowsers, SCADA systems), for which the CRA requires the manufacturer to follow a specific conformity assessment route (Module A — internal control or a harmonised standard).
- **`[AIII-II]`** — The attack targets a Class II important product (e.g., OS for servers/desktops/mobile, hypervisors, firewalls, intrusion detection/prevention systems, tamper-resistant microprocessors), for which the CRA requires a stricter conformity route (Module B+C EU-type examination, or Module H full quality assurance).

**Annex IV — Critical Products with Digital Elements**

- **`[AIV]`** — The attack targets a critical product (hardware security modules, smart meter gateways, secure elements, smartcard ICs, root CA hardware), requiring mandatory third-party conformity assessment under the strictest CRA conformity route.

A method may carry multiple labels (e.g., `[AI.I §1(c), AI.I §1(d), AI.II §2]`) where more than one specific provision is implicated. Labels identify the CRA obligations most directly relevant to the attack vector — not every conceivable regulatory touchpoint.

#### Method-perspective summary (which targets each method can reach):

- **Theft**
  - Can target: Object Specification (Part Specification), Manufacturing Process Specification, Post-Processing Specification, Indirect Manufacturing
  - Rationale: Electronic files containing all categories of AM IP can be directly stolen.

- **Reverse Engineering**
  - Can fully target: Object Specification (Part Specification)
    - Eavesdropping can reconstruct the 3D object model from toolpath commands.
    - Side-channel analysis (acoustic, thermal, EM) reconstructs the printed object's geometry.
    - Scanning (3D scanner, CMM, CT) reconstructs external shape and internal cavities.
  - Can partly target: Manufacturing Process Specification
    - Eavesdropping toolpath commands can provide insight into manufacturing process parameters.
    - Side-channel analysis of manufacturing can reveal some process characteristics.
  - Cannot target (per paper): Post-Processing Specification, Indirect Manufacturing.

- **AM Equipment Analysis — Cyber**
  - Can fully target: Manufacturing Process Specification
    - Analysis of software/firmware reveals OEM's default scanning strategy, process parameters, sensor information use, file format structure, and network protocol semantics.
  - Can partly target: Object Specification
    - File format precision and network protocol analysis can reveal some object specification aspects (e.g., resolution of geometry representation).
  - Can target: Post-Processing Specification
    - Analysis of post-processing equipment software/firmware reveals post-processing parameters.

- **AM Equipment Analysis — Physical**
  - Can fully target: Manufacturing Process Specification
    - Physical analysis of AM machine components reveals the degree to which individual manufacturing parameters can be adjusted (e.g., laser power, scan speed).
  - Can target: Post-Processing Specification
    - Physical analysis of post-processing equipment reveals its operational parameters.

- **Protection Removal**
  - Can fully target: Object Specification (Part Specification)
    - Removing watermarks or breaking device-binding from model files recovers object geometry and design information.
  - Can partly target: Manufacturing Process Specification
    - If protected model files encode process parameters (e.g., via licensing schemes), their removal may expose process information.

### 4.5 Definitions and Clarifications from the Text

#### Part Specification (§4.1.1)
- Part specification content varies greatly by AM process and material.
- For **metal AM**: files currently contain or will contain sufficient information to reproduce the full 3D geometry to meet performance requirements. Additional needed: manufacturing process specification and post-processing specification.
- For **desktop 3D printers**: files usually specify only geometry and occasionally color/material — no required properties or manufacturing process data.

#### Part Geometry (§4.1.1.1)
- Commonly specified in CAD or STL format (also AMF, 3MF, proprietary formats).
- Specifies external shape and internal cavities.
- A full specification may also include acceptable tolerances and required surface finish.
- These parameters can directly impact part mass and center of mass; if important, mass and center of mass may also be specified.

#### Required Properties (§4.1.1.2)
- Application area-optimized physical properties: mechanical, chemical, thermal, and others.
- Meeting requirements may require choosing specific AM processes and/or adjusting process parameters.
- Not present in desktop 3D printer digital blueprints.

#### Color / Texture (§4.1.1.3)
- Supported by the AMF file format (not STL).
- Irrelevant for metal AM.

#### Manufacturing Process Specification (§4.1.2)
- ASTM defines seven AM process categories; each differs in source materials, material distribution method, and heat source type.
- Unlike subtractive manufacturing, AM process parameters influence material microstructure, thus affecting physical properties. This is a key distinction.
- PBF-specific parameters include: laser power and speed, hatch spacing, background gas atmosphere, humidity in build chamber, build (Z) direction, material and location of support structures, feedstock properties, and more.
- DED-specific parameters include build direction, nozzle distance, and others.
- As of 2018, the relationship between manufacturing parameters and physical properties remains an active research area. The exact process parameter specification ensuring part quality can itself constitute IP.
- Rehme [57] lists 157 parameters arranged in an Ishikawa causal diagram.

#### Post-Processing Specification (§4.1.3)
- Required before a part can be put into service (especially metal AM).
- Includes heat treatments: annealing (controlled cooling, relieves internal stress) and HIP — Hot Isostatic Pressing (elevated temperature + high pressure, eliminates residual porosity, enhances mechanical strength, uses argon as pressurizing gas).
- Includes finish machining to remove part from build plate and improve surface finish (which significantly affects fatigue life) and fit.
- Machining methods: traditional mechanical surface finish or EDM.
- Key parameters: duration, temperature, and pressure cycle — can vary by material.
- Post-processing may be separately patentable if novel, since post-processing steps are excluded from AM-specific patents.

#### Indirect Manufacturing (§4.1.4)
- AM is used to produce tools and molds used in traditional manufacturing processes.
- Part specification (e.g., a cast for molding) enables reasoning about both the manufactured part and the process required to produce it.
- Example: molds with internal cooling passages can be created with AM to control molding cavity temperature, reduce residual stresses, and shorten cycle time.

#### Theft method (§4.2.1)
- Electronic model files can be stolen by hacking corporate networks.
- Specialized malware (e.g., ACAD/Medre.A worm) targets CAD files, copies them to foreign servers.
- AM equipment (controller PC, network-connected equipment) can also be directly compromised.
- Physical attacks and malicious insiders (direct employees or outsourced companies) are also possible.

#### Reverse Engineering method (§4.2.2)
- Distinct from theft: IP is reconstructed without direct file access.
- Can be performed during manufacturing or post-factum from the manufactured object.
- Legal status differs from theft: reverse engineering does not necessarily violate IP protection laws.
- Propagation of cheap 3D scanners accelerates this risk.
- Eavesdropping toolpath commands exchanged between controller PC and 3D printer can reconstruct the 3D model; can also reveal manufacturing process specification.
- Side-channel analysis (sound of motors, thermal imaging) reconstructs 3D object geometry.
- Scanning (hand-held 3D scanner to CMM) reconstructs external shape; CT scanning reveals internal cavities.
- Scanning introduces deviations from original IP due to measurement precision and noise.

#### AM Equipment Analysis method (§4.2.3)
- Analyzes the AM equipment itself (3D printers and post-processing equipment).
- Unlike reverse engineering from the manufactured object, this approach only reveals information about manufacturing process or post-processing — not part geometry directly.
- Cyber analysis reveals: OEM's default process specifications (e.g., scanning strategy, sensor information use in quality control), separation of responsibilities between software and firmware, which adjustments are possible.
- Physical analysis reveals: to what extent individual manufacturing parameters can be adjusted.

#### Protection Removal method (§4.2.4)
- Relevant as AM-as-a-service expands and at-home manufacturing grows.
- Digital rights management (DRM) can enforce licensing; but DRM can be disabled by dedicated effort.
- Even resilient measures integrating identifiers (rather than wrapping protection around the model) can be bypassed.
- The Digital Millennium Copyright Act of 1998 criminalizes removal of technical access-control measures; however, printed objects as tangible products are not directly protected.
- Watermarks in model files can be removed; objects printed without identifiers may serve as evidence of copyright infringement.

## 5. Taxonomy B: AM Sabotage

### 5.1 Overview

The AM sabotage taxonomy (depicted in **Fig. 7** of the paper) addresses attacks that target functional part manufacturing. Sabotage aims either to reduce a part's mechanical strength, or to reduce its fatigue life. The taxonomy distinguishes **four main attack target categories** (Manufactured Part, AM Equipment, Environment, 1st Order Causal Effects) and **six attack method categories**.

Attacks can be cross-domain (cyber-to-physical) or physical-to-physical. A singular modification often triggers a complex chain of effect propagation across cyber and physical domains.

  - Can fully target: Manufactured Part

  - Can fully target: Manufactured Part

  - Can partly target: Environment — AM Equipment
    - Compromised cyber components of a machine can carry out traditional cyberattacks on nearby systems.

- **Compromised Part**
  - Can fully target: Environment — AM Equipment
    - Parts with embedded active or passive electronic components or contaminants can attack the environment and systems into which they are integrated.
  - Can fully target: Environment — Manufactured Part
    - NBC-contaminated parts or parts with embedded electronics affect the customer upon delivery.
  - Can partly target: Manufactured Part
    - Defects introduced via supply chain into the part itself can affect the part's device properties.

### 5.5 Definitions and Clarifications from the Text

#### Manufactured Part — Fit and Form (§5.1.1.1)
- **Fit:** The ability of a part to physically interface with, connect to, or become an integral part of another part or assembly.
- **Form:** The shape, size, dimensions, mass, and visual parameters that uniquely distinguish a part.
- Effect of attack: confusion and delay — the part emerges from the machine with bad fit/form even though all inputs appeared correct, causing remaking of the part and recalibration of the machine.

#### Manufactured Part — Function (§5.1.1.2)
- In AM, material is created at the same time as the part; therefore the quality of material is also a functional concern.
- Function-relevant physical properties: physical, chemical, thermal, electrical, magnetic, optical, and mechanical properties.
- Which subset is relevant depends on: intended function, operating conditions, interactions with other parts.
- Two sub-categories of functional degradation demonstrated in research literature:
  1. Mechanical strength degraded below tolerances → destruction under normal operational conditions.
  2. Fatigue life reduced → premature failure during operation.

#### Manufactured Part — Contamination (§5.1.1.3)
- Unexpected materials and foreign objects can be embedded during the AM process.
- The manufactured part can serve as a "carrier" of NBC (nuclear, biological, chemical) contamination.

#### AM Equipment — Mechanical Components (§5.1.2.1)
- In metal AM, potential targets include: galvanometric mirrors, powder handling equipment, seals (to maintain controlled atmosphere), and re-coater systems.
- Attacks can cause excess wear (reducing lifespan) or, in extreme cases, irreparable damage.
- Example analogy: Stuxnet and the Aurora experiment demonstrated feasibility of such attacks on other industrial systems.

#### AM Equipment — Electrical Components (§5.1.2.2)
- Includes: sensors, interlocks, motors, power supplies, energy sources.
- Attacks can increase wear or cause irreparable damage.

#### AM Equipment — Cyber Infrastructure (§5.1.2.3)
- Includes: software (CAD, process modeling, slicing, toolpath generation, machine-specific instructions, process monitoring), firmware, hardware, and networks.
- Cyber infrastructure manages complex data transmission and transformation.
- Availability and integrity impacts are possible (like classical cybersecurity).
- Specific to CPS: timing disruptions can have direct impact on functionality — in AM, data delivered too early, too late, or out of order can have this impact.

#### AM Equipment — Explosion/Fire (§5.1.2.4)
- Fine metal powders can ignite or form explosive gas in combination with ambient air (oxygen content should be below 1000 ppm).
- Use of fine powders opens potential for inducing fire or explosion in the equipment.

#### Environment — AM Equipment (§5.1.3.1)
- Attack on AM equipment could affect other nearby equipment: powder sieving equipment, feedstock storage.
- Contaminated feedstock could affect the entire supply chain infrastructure and machine environment.
- Compromised electrical components could damage the machine and have broader implications for the power source.
- Compromised cyber components could carry out traditional cyberattacks on other systems.

#### Environment — Manufactured Part (§5.1.3.2)
- A defective part could fail in-service when integrated into final assembly.
- Example: embedding a defect in a rotating part to fail at a predetermined speed (demonstrated in dr0wned study).
- Embedded active or passive electronic components, or contaminants, could affect the customer upon delivery.
- For military equipment: malicious modifications can cause EM, heat, or vibration emanations, or alter the reflectivity of the CPS, reducing stealthiness without necessarily affecting the part's primary function.

#### 1st Order Causal Effects (§5.1.4)
- These are **consequences** of sabotage attacks, not primary attack targets. The paper explicitly states: "While the effects of sabotage attacks are not the focus of this taxonomy, they inform the motivations of attackers."
- Economically motivated attacks focus on cost efficiency and acceptance rate.
- Military/strategic attacks focus on manufacturing availability.
- A full examination of sabotage effects moves beyond AM as a discipline into security, strategy, and risk evaluation.

#### 3D Part Specification — Part Geometry (§5.2.1.1)
- Malicious modification can affect fit and form.
- Defects can be inserted in the internals of the 3D structure — an essential difference from subtractive manufacturing, where geometry changes are discernible via direct measurement.
- For AM, defects can be small enough to evade detection even by CT scan.
- Changes to size, geometry, and position of internal cavities impact mass, center of mass, and function (mechanical, thermal, other properties).

#### 3D Part Specification — Required Properties (§5.2.1.2)
- Required functional properties may depend on a combination of manufacturing parameters (e.g., scanning strategy).
- Changing the required properties specification may cause changes in manufacturing parameters selected.
- Defects introduced can alter failure behavior and useful lifetime.

#### 3D Part Specification — Source Material (§5.2.1.3)
- For multi-material AM processes, substituting incorrect materials is a viable manipulation.
- Differences between original and contaminant material affect mechanical, thermal, and other properties.

#### Supply Chain Attack — Feedstock (§5.2.2.1)
- Feedstock is purchased from a vendor who may not be the producer.
- Characterization is expensive and time-consuming → motivation to accept without characterization.
- Performance can be altered by changing particle size distribution, disrupting the process.
- Vulnerable during recycling (blending and sieving) operations.
- Susceptible to introduction of contaminants including oxygen, moisture, and NBC.

#### Supply Chain Attack — Replacement Parts (§5.2.2.2)
- Software, firmware, mechanical, electrical, and electronic components are replaced during failure or upgrades.
- Subtle changes in machine control software can profoundly affect part properties.
- In AM production, no software upgrades are allowed without machine requalification (which can take months).
- However, malware can employ complex activation triggers to bypass the requalification process.

#### Supply Chain Attack — AM Equipment (§5.2.2.3)
- AM machines can be delivered in a compromised state (compromised software, firmware, hardware).
- Analogous to compromised delivery of any CPS.

#### Supply Chain Attack — Manufactured Object (§5.2.2.4)
- Supply chain attacks on manufactured objects can include: replacement (to impair fit or function), NBC contamination, or cyber infection of embedded electronics.

#### AM Manufacturing Process — Process Control (§5.2.3.1)
- More than 130 parameters govern metal AM processes; not all equally important.
- Laser scanning strategy is one of the most critical: for each slice, 100% of the volume must be melted and solidified without defects. Changes can induce lack of fusion or keyhole defects. Scanning strategy includes laser path, power, and speed.
- Build direction affects anisotropic properties — altering it can alter expected part properties.
- Number of parts on a build plate or powder spreading speed changes part thermal history and therefore properties.
- Laser delay settings may be manipulated to create microscopic leaks in thin-wall vessels.

#### AM Manufacturing Process — Sensor Information (§5.2.3.2)
- Manipulating sensor information enables false state estimation attacks on CPS employing control loops.
- An indirect sabotage attack via in-situ IR thermography has been demonstrated: false sensor readings caused the control system to adjust laser energy incorrectly, inducing porosity defects.
- False sensor readings of oxygen content or pressure in the build chamber can lead to violation of safety requirements.

#### Post-Processing — Heat Treatment (§5.2.4.1)
- Heat treatment is carried out to relax residual stresses and impart desirable mechanical properties.
- Alterations to heat treatment parameters can adversely affect both objectives.

#### Post-Processing — Finish Machining (§5.2.4.2)
- Finish machining is usually required to achieve desired fit and function.
- Security issues in this area are the same as for subtractive manufacturing.

#### Compromised AM Equipment (§5.2.5)
- Enables several attack methods including: 3D Part Specification, AM Manufacturing Process, and Post-Processing.
- Equipment includes not only 3D printers but also CT scanners (quality control), firmware, software (directly/indirectly in toolchain), and computer networks.
- Compromised software can alter the object model "on the fly" (firmware) or during processing steps (FEA, distortion correction).
- Malicious FEA software could report acceptable performance when a simulated part has a defect.
- If a model is altered "on the fly" by firmware, quality control must also be compromised to avoid detection.
- Compromised quality control can also be used to falsely identify good parts as deficient (efficiency attack).

#### Compromised Part (§5.2.6)
- Similar to Compromised AM Equipment: manufactured parts with embedded items or contamination can be used for active attacks on their environment.

## 6. Taxonomy Notes and Interpretation Constraints

### 6.1 Scope of the taxonomy (natural sciences only)
The authors explicitly restrict their taxonomy to "aspects covered by relevant natural science domains." This means:
- Included: cybersecurity aspects (attack vectors, compromised elements, manipulations), materials science aspects (microstructure, properties), mechanical engineering aspects (fatigue, strength).
- Excluded: socioeconomic motivations, political dimensions, legal aspects, organizational countermeasures. These are discussed in the paper's survey section (Section 6) but are not part of the taxonomy itself.

### 6.2 1st Order Causal Effects are not the primary taxonomy focus
The 1st Order Causal Effects branch appears in Fig. 7 but is explicitly described by the authors as **consequences** of sabotage attacks rather than primary attack targets:
> "While the effects of sabotage attacks are not the focus of this taxonomy, they inform the motivations of attackers."
This branch is included to support motivation analysis and attacker profiling, not as a technical attack target in the same sense as Manufactured Part, AM Equipment, or Environment.

### 6.3 Narrower scope than other AM security taxonomies
The authors explicitly note that all contemporaneous AM security taxonomies (Pan et al., Wu et al., Gupta et al., Yampolskiy et al. prior work) have broader scope, operate at higher abstraction levels, and use different organizing principles. This taxonomy is **narrower in scope** but **significantly greater in depth**.

### 6.4 Ellipsis nodes in the figures
Both Fig. 5 and Fig. 7 contain explicit ellipsis ("...") nodes at multiple levels in the tree. These indicate that the taxonomy is intentionally open-ended at those points — additional subcategories are implied by the domain but not exhaustively enumerated. This document preserves ellipsis markers to faithfully represent this intentional incompleteness.

### 6.5 Illegal Part Manufacturing is explicitly excluded
Despite being listed in Fig. 4 as a major threat category (Export Controlled Parts, Internationally Prohibited Parts), the authors state:
> "...there are no security-related technical differences between illegal manufacturing using AM or using traditional CNC machines. Because of a lack of technical differences for attacks, we explicitly exclude the latter threat category from consideration in this paper."

### 6.6 Consequences vs. targets distinction
The paper distinguishes between:
- **Attack targets** = direct effects that align with adversarial goals (primary taxonomy focus).
- **Consequences / causal effects** = downstream effects that may motivate attackers but move beyond AM as a discipline (noted in taxonomy but not the structural focus).

### 6.7 Attack method applies regardless of compromised element
A critical design choice: the same attack method (e.g., "modify part geometry") is taxonomically unified even if it can be achieved through different compromised elements (controller PC, internal network, firmware). The compromised element is intentionally abstracted away in the taxonomy to provide a semantically clean conceptual view.

### 6.8 Properties affectable by AM process (Table 1 in paper)
The paper provides Table 1 summarizing which physical, chemical, thermal, electrical, magnetic, optical, and mechanical properties **can** be affected by the AM process. This informs the Function sub-target under Manufactured Part. Key findings:
- **Yes (can be affected):** Density, Shape and size, Corrosion resistance, Coercivity, Permeability, Remanence, Magnetostriction, Ductility, Toughness, Yield strength.
- **Possibly (conditionally):** Chemical composition, Thermal conductivity, Electrical conductivity, Diffuse scattering, Brittleness, Elasticity, Fatigue.
- **No:** Color, Melting point, Thermal expansion, Specific heat, Temperature coefficient of resistance, Dielectric strength, Thermoelectricity, Refractive index, Emissivity, Absorptivity, Creep.
